How many? The arithmetic of campus sexual assault

Four serious attempts to count how often college women are sexually assaulted. Two of them come from the same federal agency. Their answers differ by an order of magnitude — and much of the difference is built into the instruments before a single student answers a question.

Two of these instruments belong to the same agency. The Bureau of Justice Statistics runs the National Crime Victimization Survey, which puts the annual rate for female students at 6.1 per 1,000. The same bureau, working with RTI International, ran a nine-school pilot that put the share of undergraduate women sexually assaulted in a single academic year at 10.3 percent — 103 per 1,000. The bureau did not change its mind between the two. It changed the instrument.

The meta-analysis pools studies with different reference periods; its authors report heterogeneity above 97 percent, meaning the studies disagree far more than sampling error alone would explain.

How noisy is the underlying literature? Pool the 97 studies of women in that meta-analysis by recall period and the past-year estimate comes out at 18.0 percent — higher than the 16.8 percent estimate for a student's entire time in higher education. A single year cannot contain more than several years. The inversion is not a finding about students; it is a measurement of how far apart the studies are.
The comparison to avoid. A cumulative rate — "since enrolling" — covers up to four years and climbs with every year a student stays. An academic-year rate covers about nine months. Setting one against the other without saying which is which is the most common error in this literature, and it is available to both sides of the argument.
02 — Definitions

One survey, one population, five defensible answers

Everything below comes from a single survey of a single group — undergraduate women in the AAU's 2019 study, asked about their time since enrolling. Each figure is one the AAU itself published. What changes is only which behaviors and which tactics you decide to count.

12.8%
Any penetration or attempted penetration, by force or inability to consent. The AAU notes this category “meets the legal definitions for rape in many jurisdictions.”
“undergraduate women had a prevalence rate of 12.8 percent compared to 4.6 percent of women graduate/professional students.”

All figures: AAU 2019 report, pp. 14–15, undergraduate women, since enrolling at the school. These are the AAU's own published subtotals, not sums calculated here, and the categories overlap — one student can appear in several.

This is why two people can quote the same study and appear to be describing different countries. A commentator who says one in four is citing the widest category. A commentator who says one in eight is citing the category the survey itself associates with the legal definition of rape. A commentator who says roughly one in fourteen is citing forcible penetration. None of them is inventing a number.

The second lever is who answers. The AAU survey reached 21.9 percent of the students it invited — 26.1 percent of women — and school-level response ranged from 6 percent to 68 percent. The federal validation study reached 54 percent of the women it invited. Low response does not automatically mean an estimate is too high; it means the band of uncertainty is wider in both directions, a point the AAU's own report makes plainly.

If non-respondents are more likely to be victimized, then there will be a negative bias in the estimates (i.e., the estimated victimization rate will be too low). If non-respondents are less likely to be victimized, then estimates are too high. Report on the AAU Campus Climate Survey on Sexual Assault and Misconduct, 2019, p. 6
03 — Variation

The same questionnaire, nine campuses, one in two to one in eight

The federal validation study fielded an identical instrument, in the same weeks of 2015, at nine institutions. Among fourth-year women asked whether they had experienced unwanted or nonconsensual sexual contact since entering college, the answer ranged from 13 percent at one school to 51 percent at another.

The study attaches an explicit caution to these cumulative figures: no incident-level detail was collected for victimizations before the 2014–15 academic year, and a reference period of roughly 3.5 years is more vulnerable to recall error and telescoping. The better-validated academic-year figures from the same nine schools vary almost as widely: 4.2 percent to 20.0 percent.

Both of the numbers that get quoted nationally — the one in four and the six in a thousand — are averages taken across places that do not resemble one another. Within this single study, run to a single protocol, the spread from the lowest campus to the highest is wider than the gap between the two studies that are usually set against each other.

04 — The official record

Of every hundred rapes the survey found, about three could ever appear in the federal count

Source: BJS/RTI Campus Climate Survey Validation Study, p. 110. The bureau's own caveats apply: Clery covers all students and employees while the survey covered undergraduates; some victimized students may have left school; and the two collections define rape, campus geography and reporting officials somewhat differently.

That convergence matters in both directions. It is evidence that Clery numbers are not being fabricated — the schools reported roughly what the survey predicted they would. It is also evidence that Clery numbers cannot be read as a measure of how much sexual assault happens, because the law was never built to capture it. As the bureau put it: the vast majority of rapes are not reported to authorities and are not represented in an institution's Clery numbers.

Across the 33 schools, women contacted a program in 29.5 percent of penetration incidents — ranging from 16.5 percent at one school to 65.6 percent at another. In the federal validation study, 12.5 percent of rape incidents were reported to any official.

Among students who did report, most found the response useful: in the validation study, victims considered the official helpful in 81 percent of reported rape incidents. The barrier that shows up largest in the data is not a belief that the system will fail. It is a judgment about the seriousness of what happened — which is, once again, a question about definitions.

05 — Consequences

For the women who are counted, the effects are still measurable a decade later

The largest recent study of what happens afterward is population-scale and Finnish. Researchers linked every police report and hospital admission for rape between 2006 and 2019 to national records of employment, earnings, education and prescriptions, then compared each victim with observationally equivalent women who were not assaulted.

Source: Adams, Huttunen, Nix & Zhang, “The Economic Impacts of Rape,” IFS Working Paper 26/15, February 2026. Finland, 2006–2019. A matched-control event study, not an experiment; the comparison group is observationally equivalent women, and the estimates cover assaults that reached a police or hospital record.

The finding most relevant to a campus is what happens to the youngest victims. Women raped as adolescents were, by the age of 25, 10 percentage points less likely to have graduated from college and 14 points less likely to be employed than young women with the same pre-assault grades, mental health and family circumstances. The authors note that the drop in college completion is sharpest among the most academically able teenage victims — the students who, on every prior indicator, were most likely to finish.

The harm also does not stop at the person. When a girl in a Finnish school cohort was raped, her female classmates' antidepressant prescriptions rose measurably — the equivalent of one additional classmate on medication. There was no such effect among male classmates.

What this evidence is and is not. It is population-scale, administrative and longitudinal, which is rare. It is also Finnish, and it captures assaults that entered an official record. Campus-specific work is weaker by design: a systematic review of thirteen studies reports that across all of them, sexual assault was associated with “more academic problems including lower grade point average, dropping out of university, and self-regulated learning problems” — associations drawn from observational data, not causal estimates, and the authors note the number of studies is small even as the results are consistent.

Sources: Adams et al. (2026); Molstad, Weinhardt & Jones, Trauma, Violence & Abuse (2023). In the AAU survey, 15.1 percent of women reported physical injuries from incidents involving penetration.

Enrolled women are not at higher risk than their peers who are not in college

The national lifetime figures place the college years inside the period of highest risk rather than apart from it. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reports that more than one in five women have experienced completed or attempted rape in their lifetimes, and that nearly half of women have experienced some form of contact sexual violence. Most of it begins early: more than four in five female rape survivors were first raped before the age of 25, and almost half before 18 — that is, before the traditional college years begin.

That is the context a campus figure sits inside. A number covering ages 18 to 22 is measuring the years in which risk is highest for everyone in that age group, enrolled or not, and it is measuring a population some of whom arrived already having been assaulted. The federal cost estimate the CDC cites for a single rape — medical care, lost productivity, criminal justice — is $122,461 per survivor.

Source: CDC, About Sexual Violence, updated December 2025, drawing on the National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey. Lifetime figures from the CDC and annual figures from the crime survey come from different instruments and cannot be added together.

07 — The audience

Seventy percent of Americans say higher education is going the wrong way

Pew did not ask about sexual assault, and nothing in this survey attributes the decline to campus safety. It is included here for one reason: it describes the audience. Campus climate results are published into a country where seven in ten adults, across both parties, already say the system is headed the wrong way.

What follows from the measurement choices. Each choice on this page has a direction. Widening the definitions, lengthening the recall period and surveying the whole student body about sexual misconduct all push a published figure up, without anything changing on the campus itself. Tightening them pushes it down. That is why the same institution can produce two very different numbers in the same year, and why the questions worth asking of any figure are: what was counted, over what period, of whom, and how many answered.
