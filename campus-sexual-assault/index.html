How many? The arithmetic of campus sexual assault

Three large surveys and one administrative count try to measure how often college women are sexually assaulted. Two of the surveys come from the same federal agency. Their answers differ by more than an order of magnitude, and much of the difference comes from how each instrument is built: what it asks, over what period and of whom.

Two of these instruments belong to the same agency. The Bureau of Justice Statistics runs the National Crime Victimization Survey, which puts the rate of completed rape for female students at 2.0 victimizations per 1,000 a year. The same bureau, working with RTI International, ran a nine-school pilot that counted 54 completed rapes per 1,000 undergraduate women in part of a single academic year, and found that 4.1 percent of those women had been raped. Between the two, the questions changed. So did the schools, the years, the interview mode and the response rates.

One study changed only the questions. In the spring of 1997, Bonnie Fisher, Francis Cullen and Michael Turner surveyed two national samples of college women by telephone, about the same academic year. The main sample of 4,446 heard screening questions that described specific sexual acts. A comparison sample of 4,432 heard the screening questions and incident report used by the crime survey. The share reporting a completed rape was 1.7 percent in the main sample and 0.16 percent in the comparison sample, 11 times smaller. The authors concluded that, if their own method was defensible, “it seems likely that NCVS underestimates the true incidence of rape victimization.”

The meta-analysis is international and pools studies with different reference periods; its authors report heterogeneity above 97 percent, meaning the studies disagree far more than sampling error alone would explain.

How far apart are the studies? Split the meta-analysis's estimates for women by recall period and the past-year figure, from 47 estimates, comes out at 18.0 percent. The figure for a student's entire time in higher education, from 50 largely different estimates, is 16.8 percent. Within one survey, a single year could not exceed several. Across these pooled studies it does, slightly, and the two confidence intervals overlap. The comparison says more about the spread of the literature than about students.
The comparison to avoid. A cumulative rate — "since enrolling" — covers up to four years and climbs with every year a student stays. An academic-year rate covers the months from the start of the fall term to the date of the survey. Setting one against the other without saying which is which is the most common error in this literature, and it is available to both sides of the argument.
02 — Definitions

One survey, one population, six defensible answers

Everything below comes from a single survey of a single group: undergraduate women in the AAU's 2019 study, asked about their time since enrolling. Each figure is one the AAU itself published. What changes is which behaviors and which tactics you decide to count.

12.8%
Any completed or attempted penetration, by force, inability to consent or both. The AAU notes this category “meets the legal definitions for rape in many jurisdictions.”
“undergraduate women had a prevalence rate of 12.8 percent compared to 4.6 percent of women graduate/professional students.”

All figures: AAU 2019 report, pp. 14–15, and appendix table A7-5, undergraduate women, since enrolling at the school. These are the AAU's own published subtotals, not sums calculated here. They should not be added together: one student can appear in several categories.

This is why two people can quote the same study and appear to be describing different countries. A commentator who says one in four is citing the broadest force-or-incapacitation category. A commentator who says one in eight is citing the category the survey itself associates with the legal definition of rape. A commentator who says roughly one in fourteen is citing penetration by physical force alone, which leaves out the 2.5 percent who reported force and incapacitation together. None of them is inventing a number.

The second lever is who answers. The AAU survey reached 21.9 percent of the students it invited, including 26.1 percent of women, and school-level response ranged from 6 percent to 68 percent. The federal validation study reached an average of 54 percent of the women it invited. A low response rate raises the risk of bias, which can run in either direction. Both surveys tested for it. The validation study compared respondents with nonrespondents on school records and found “little evidence of nonresponse bias,” and students who answered late in the field period reported sexual assault at the same rate as those who answered early. The AAU found a small upward bias, and “little evidence that non-response bias can explain the high rates of victimization.”

Analysis of the 2015 data found some evidence that the non-response is in a positive direction—that is the published estimates were too high, although not by a large amount. Report on the AAU Campus Climate Survey on Sexual Assault and Misconduct, 2019, p. 7; see also p. xvii and appendix 4. Validation study: BJS/RTI CCSVS, pp. ES-5 and 50.
03 — Variation

The same questionnaire, nine campuses, one in two to one in eight

The federal validation study fielded an identical instrument at nine institutions in the spring of 2015. Source: BJS/RTI Campus Climate Survey Validation Study, p. 76, and appendix tables E-10 to E-18 for standard errors; intervals are each estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors. The study's published average of 25.1 percent covers only its four-year schools. It equals the average of the eight schools other than School 2. The study also reports that School 2 did not reach its target sample size. The study attaches an explicit caution to these cumulative figures: no incident-level detail was collected for victimizations before the 2014–15 academic year, and a reference period of roughly 3.5 years is more vulnerable to recall error and telescoping. The academic-year figures from the same nine schools range from 4.2 percent to 20.0 percent.

The AAU's 25.9 percent is itself an average across 33 schools, whose rates for undergraduate women range from 14.0 percent to 32.0 percent (AAU 2019, p. 17). Within this single study, run to a single protocol, each school's confidence interval spans roughly 4 to 9 percentage points. The distance from the lowest school to the highest is 37.6 points. Most of the spread is larger than sampling error can explain.

The bureau's own caveats apply: Clery covers all students and employees while the survey covered undergraduates; some victimized students may have left school; and the two collections define rape, campus geography and reporting officials somewhat differently.

The two counts were close: the schools reported roughly what the survey predicted would reach them. Most rapes the survey found happened off campus or were never reported to school authorities, so they fall outside what Clery counts. As the bureau put it: the vast majority of rapes are not reported to authorities and are not represented in an institution's Clery numbers.

05 — Consequences

For victims whose cases reached police or hospitals, the effects lasted at least a decade

A 2026 Finnish working paper, not yet published in a journal, measures what happens afterward across a whole country. Researchers linked police reports and hospital admissions for rape and related sexual offenses between 2006 and 2019 to national records of employment, earnings, education and prescriptions, then compared each victim with observationally equivalent women who were not assaulted.

Source: Adams, Huttunen, Nix & Zhang, “The Economic Impacts of Rape,” IFS Working Paper 26/15, February 2026. Finland, 2006–2019. A matched-control event study, not an experiment; the comparison group is observationally equivalent women. The paper's “rape” category is broader than the legal term: half the police cases are rape or aggravated rape, 41 percent are sexual abuse, coercion into a sexual act or sexual harassment, and the rest are attempts. Cases that reach police or hospitals may be more severe than many incidents counted in campus surveys, so these effects should not be applied to survey totals.

The finding most relevant to a campus is what happens to the youngest victims. Women raped as adolescents were, by the age of 25, 10 percentage points less likely to have graduated from college and 14 points less likely to be employed than young women with the same pre-assault grades, mental health and family circumstances. The authors note that the drop in college completion is sharpest among the most academically able teenage victims — the students who, on every prior indicator, were most likely to finish.

The harm also does not stop at the person. When an adolescent girl was assaulted, antidepressant prescriptions rose among her female classmates. The authors put the size at roughly one additional girl on antidepressants for every five classes in which a girl was assaulted. They found no effect among male classmates.

What this evidence is and is not. It is population-scale, administrative and longitudinal, which is rare. It is also Finnish, it has not yet been through journal review, and it captures assaults that entered an official record. Campus-specific work is weaker by design: a systematic review of thirteen studies reports that across all of them, sexual assault was associated with “more academic problems including lower grade point average, dropping out of university, and self-regulated learning problems” — associations drawn from observational data, not causal estimates, and the authors note the number of studies is small even as the results are consistent.

Sources: Adams et al. (2026); Molstad, Weinhardt & Jones, Trauma, Violence & Abuse (2023). In the AAU survey, women reported physical injuries in 15.1 percent of incidents involving penetration (AAU 2019, p. 28 and table 20).

06 — Beyond campus

In the crime survey, enrolled women were not at higher risk than their peers outside college

Source: BJS, NCJ 248471, table 1, covering 1995–2013. One survey, one definition, one period, which is what makes the comparison usable. The difference in total rates is statistically significant at the 90 percent confidence level. “Students” includes women enrolled full or part time in a college, university, trade school or vocational school. By 2013 the rates had converged, at about 4.3 per 1,000 for both groups. The comparison inherits the crime survey's own measurement: student victimizations were less likely to be reported to police, and the survey cannot show whether students were also less likely to describe an incident to an interviewer as a crime.

The college years fall inside the period of highest risk. From 1997 to 2013, according to the Bureau of Justice Statistics, women ages 18 to 24 consistently had higher rates of rape and sexual assault than women in any other age group. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reports that more than one in five women have experienced completed or attempted rape in their lifetimes, and that nearly half of women have experienced some form of contact sexual violence. Most of it begins early: more than four in five female rape survivors were first raped before the age of 25, and almost half before 18, before the traditional college years begin.

A campus figure covering ages 18 to 22 measures the years in which risk is highest for everyone in that age group, enrolled or not, in a population some of whom arrived having already been assaulted. The CDC puts the lifetime cost of rape, including medical care, lost productivity and criminal justice costs, at $122,461 per survivor.

Sources: BJS, NCJ 248471, figure 2; CDC, About Sexual Violence, updated December 2025, drawing on the National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey. Lifetime figures from the CDC and annual figures from the crime survey come from different instruments and cannot be added together.

What follows from the measurement choices. Each choice on this page has a direction. Broader definitions, longer recall periods, screening questions that describe specific acts and surveys framed around sexual misconduct tend to push a published figure up. A survey framed around crime, screening questions that do not describe specific acts and a bounded six-month recall period tend to push it down. Neither set of choices is neutral, and none of them changes anything on a campus. The questions worth asking of any figure: what was counted, over what period, of whom, and how many answered.
