# Campus sexual assault: how the numbers are made — data and sources

**Purpose.** This is the companion ledger to `index.html`. Every figure in the story appears here with
the exact source, the supporting language from that source (quoted), and any complexity a careful
reader should weigh. Nothing appears on the page that is not in this file.

**Rule used throughout.** No figure is published unless it was read in the primary document — the
federal report, the peer-reviewed paper or the agency table itself. Where a number is derived
(arithmetic performed here rather than published by the source), it is labeled *derived* both here
and on the page.

**Maintainer note.** When a number changes in `index.html`, change it here too.

## Hero

**"6.1 per 1,000" (NCVS annual rate, female students 18–24).**
Source: [BJS, *Rape and Sexual Assault Victimization Among College-Age Females, 1995–2013*, NCJ 248471](https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/rape-and-sexual-assault-among-college-age-females-1995-2013), Sinozich & Langton — "The rate of rape and sexual assault was 1.2 times higher for nonstudents (7.6 per 1,000) than for students (6.1 per 1,000)."
*Complexities:* This is an **incidence** rate — victimizations per 1,000 persons per year, averaged over 1995–2013 — not the share of women victimized. It is not directly comparable to a prevalence percentage.

**"10.3%" (CCSVS academic-year prevalence, undergraduate women).**
Source: [CCSVS Final Technical Report, BJS/RTI, January 2016](https://bjs.ojp.gov/content/pub/pdf/ccsvsftr.pdf) — "The prevalence rate for completed sexual assault experienced by undergraduate females during the 2014–2015 academic year, averaged across the nine schools, was 10.3%, and ranged from 4.2% at School 2 to 20.0% at School 1."

**"25.9%" (AAU cumulative prevalence, undergraduate women).**
Source: [AAU 2019 report, p. 14](https://www.aau.edu/sites/default/files/AAU-Files/Key-Issues/Campus-Safety/Revised%20Aggregate%20report%20%20and%20appendices%201-7_(01-16-2020_FINAL).pdf) — "the estimate for women undergraduates is between two and three times higher at 25.9 percent than women graduate and professional students, at 9.7 percent."
*Complexities:* Cumulative — "since enrolling in the school," not annual. Covers nonconsensual penetration, attempted penetration or sexual touching by physical force or inability to consent.

## Section 1 — Four rulers

### The shared axis (annual rate per 1,000 women)

| Marker | Value plotted | Published figure | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clery / federal campus crime reports | 0.31 per 1,000 students (**derived**) | 5,839 reported on-campus rapes, 2021 | [NCES Digest table 329.10](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_329.10.asp) |
| NCVS | 6.1 per 1,000 | 6.1 per 1,000 female students 18–24 | BJS NCJ 248471 |
| CCSVS rape | 41 per 1,000 | 4.1% academic-year rape prevalence | CCSVS, p. ES-6 |
| CCSVS sexual assault | 103 per 1,000 | 10.3% academic-year prevalence | CCSVS, p. ES-6 |
| AAU, first-year women | 161 per 1,000 | 16.1% in the current academic year | AAU 2019, p. 16 |

*Derivation, Clery marker:* 5,839 on-campus rapes reported in calendar 2021 ÷ 18.6 million students
(15.4M undergraduate + 3.2M postbaccalaureate, fall 2021, [NCES Fast Facts](https://nces.ed.gov/fastfacts/display.asp?id=98)) × 1,000 = **0.31 per 1,000**. This is the only derived number on the axis.

*Complexities — read before comparing:*
1. **Incidence vs. prevalence.** The NCVS marker counts *victimizations*; the CCSVS and AAU markers count *women who were victimized at least once*. Because victims are often victimized more than once, a prevalence rate is lower than an incidence rate in the same population — so the true methodological gap is somewhat smaller than the raw axis distance, though still roughly an order of magnitude.
2. **The Clery marker is not a victimization rate.** It counts incidents reported to campus authorities that meet Clery's geography rules (on campus and certain adjacent/associated property), for students and non-students of any gender. Most incidents are outside its scope by construction. It belongs on the axis as the administrative floor, not as a competing estimate of how often assault happens.
3. **Different populations.** NCVS is nationally representative of 18–24-year-old female students; CCSVS is nine schools; AAU is 33 large, mostly private research universities.
4. **Different years.** 1995–2013 (NCVS), 2014–15 (CCSVS), 2019 (AAU), 2021 (Clery).

### AAU response rate

Source: AAU 2019, p. 5 — "The final response rate was 21.9 percent (Table 2). This rate varied by gender (17.5% men, 26.1% women) and affiliation status (20.4% undergraduates, 24.5% graduate and professional students)." Also: 181,752 students at 33 schools; school-level response rates ranged "from a low of 6 percent to a high of 68.0 percent."

### CCSVS response rate

Source: AAU 2019, p. 44 — "The response rate was 54 percent for women and 40 percent for men. The comparable rates for AAU were 26.1 and 17.5 percent, respectively."

### AAU's own comparison to CCSVS

Source: AAU 2019, p. 45 — "The CCVS estimate for undergraduate women being a victim of nonconsensual contact since enrolled (20.5%) is somewhat below the AAU estimate (24.9%), although it is well within the same range... the AAU rates ranged from 14.0 percent to 32.0 percent, compared to a range of 13.0 percent to 37.0 percent for the CCVS."
*Note:* AAU's report abbreviates the validation study "CCVS"; BJS calls it CCSVS. The 24.9% AAU figure here is the 2015 survey, matched in time to CCSVS.

### Meta-analysis pooled estimates

Source: [Steele, Martin, Sciarra, Melendez-Torres, Degli Esposti & Humphreys, Trauma, Violence & Abuse (2024)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/15248380231196119) — 131 articles from 21 countries. Pooled prevalence: women 17.5% (95% CI 15.3–20.0); men 7.8% (5.7–10.5); transgender/gender-diverse 18.1% (13.7–23.5). By type for women: forced sexual touching 14%; coercive sex 11.2%; attempted rape 8.2%; rape 5.9%. "All meta-analyses had considerable heterogeneity (I² > 97%)."
Verbatim: "We identified 131 articles, from 21 different countries. The meta-analyzed prevalence of sexual assault was 17.5% for women, 7.8% for men, and 18.1% for transgender and gender diverse people." And: "17.5% of women experiencing any type of SA ([15.3, 20.0]...)" and "All meta-analyses had considerable heterogeneity (I² > 97%)."

**The recall-period inversion (page, section 1).** From the paper's Table 4, *Meta-Analyzed Prevalence of SA Victimization Stratified by Recall Period and Type of SA*, women, overall:

| Recall period | Pooled prevalence | 95% CI | Studies |
|---|---|---|---|
| Past year | 0.180 — shown on the page as 18.0 percent | .148–.216 | 47 |
| Entire time at a higher-education institution | 0.168 — shown on the page as 16.8 percent | .138–.203 | 50 |
| Overall | 0.175 | .153–.20 | 97 |

Rape, women: past year 0.051; entire time 0.070; overall **0.059** (95% CI .042–.080), 58 studies.

*Complexities:* The past-year pooled figure exceeding the entire-time figure is logically impossible within one population and instrument; it reflects that these are different sets of studies (47 and 50, largely different papers) with different samples and measures, not a claim about students. The confidence intervals overlap. The page presents it as an indicator of dispersion in the literature, which is what it is. Reference periods differ across the pooled studies — the authors note "there is discrepancy in the time in which experiences of SA are asked about." A pooled figure across mixed reference periods is a summary of a literature, not a national estimate. I² > 97% means the studies disagree far more than sampling error alone would explain.

*Verification note:* the SAGE page became unreadable to automated fetching during checking; these figures were confirmed a second time from the open-access full text at [Europe PMC, PMC11155219](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC11155219).

## Section 2 — The definitions dial

All five values are AAU 2019, undergraduate women, since enrolling at the school. They are AAU's own
published subtotals — **not sums computed here**, and they overlap (a woman can appear in more than one).

| Definition | Rate | Source language (AAU 2019, pp. 15) |
|---|---|---|
| Penetration by physical force | 7.3% | "Among undergraduate women, 7.3 percent reported penetration by physical force" |
| Penetration by inability to consent | 5.4% | "and 5.4 percent reported penetration related to an inability to consent" |
| Both force and inability to consent | 2.5% | "Instances of physical force and inability to consent together were reported by 2.5 percent of undergraduate women" |
| Any penetration or attempted penetration, by force or inability to consent | 12.8% | "undergraduate women had a prevalence rate of 12.8 percent compared to 4.6 percent of women graduate/professional students" |
| Sexual touching by force or inability to consent | 19.6% | "among women, 19.6 percent of undergraduates reported sexual touching compared to 6.9 percent of graduates/professionals" |
| Any of the above (nonconsensual sexual contact) | 25.9% | See hero note |

AAU's own framing of the legal threshold, p. 15: "Acts involving penetration or attempted penetration by physical force or inability to consent meet the legal definitions for rape in many jurisdictions."

*Complexities:* The tactic "inability to consent" is defined in the survey by the respondent's own
judgment that they were "unable to consent or stop what was happening." State criminal statutes set a
higher and more specific bar, and several states exclude voluntary intoxication from their definition
of incapacity. Survey categories and criminal categories are not the same thing, and the page says so.

## Section 4 — What reaches the record

### The nine-school funnel

Source: CCSVS, p. 110 (sidebar, "The Clery Act and Reporting Campus Rape") — "male and female undergraduate students at the nine institutions participating in the CCSVS experienced a total of 2,380 incidents of completed rape during the 2014–15 academic year, of which 770 (32%) occurred on campus. According to the CCSVS data, about 60 of these rape incidents (margin of error between 30 and 90 incidents) were committed on campus and reported to school authorities. In other words, approximately 3% of all completed rapes captured by the CCSVS would be expected to be included under Clery reporting standards... the CCSVS estimate (60 rape incidents) was not statistically different from the Clery number of rapes reported (40 rape incidents)."

BJS's conclusion, same page: "the CCSVS data suggest that the vast majority of rapes are not reported to authorities and are not represented in an institution's Clery numbers."

*Complexities:* BJS lists its own caveats for the comparison — Clery covers all students and employees while CCSVS covers undergraduates; some victimized students may have left school; and there are definitional differences (what counts as rape, what counts as on campus, who is a reporting official).

### Reporting rates

- CCSVS, p. 107: "Across all nine schools, 4.3% of sexual battery incidents and 12.5% of rape incidents were reported by the victim to any official."
- AAU 2019, p. 28: "For incidents involving penetration, women contacted a program for 29.5 percent of incidents, TGQN students for 42.9 percent of incidents, and men for 17.8 percent of incidents... The proportion of incidents involving penetration in which the victim contacted a program across the 33 schools varies from 16.5 to 65.6 percent."
- AAU 2019, p. 30, reasons given by women for not contacting a program after penetration incidents: could handle it themselves 48.8%; not serious enough 47.4%; embarrassed, ashamed or too emotionally difficult 41.7%; did not want to get the perpetrator in trouble 24.5%; did not think resources could help 21.9%.
- CCSVS, p. 109: officials were considered helpful for "81% of rape incidents and 79% of sexual battery incidents that were reported to any official."

### National administrative count

Source: [NCES Digest of Education Statistics, table 329.10](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_329.10.asp), from the Department of Education's Campus Safety and Security Reporting System — reported on-campus rapes, all institutions: 4,431 (2014); 5,119 (2015); 5,840 (2016); 6,583 (2017); 6,682 (2018); 6,187 (2019); 5,164 (2020); 5,839 (2021).
*Verification note:* the row was parsed directly from the table's HTML rather than read off a rendered page, because the 2021 column splits into "Total" and "In residence halls" and is easy to misread. For 2021 the total is 5,839, of which 4,716 were in residence halls. The "rape" category begins in 2014, when the Clery definitions changed; earlier years show "---".
*Complexities:* 2020 and 2021 counts cover academic terms disrupted by COVID-19 closures, when far fewer students were on campus. Year-over-year changes in this series are not evidence about the underlying rate of assault.

## Section 5 — What follows for victims

### Adams, Huttunen, Nix & Zhang, *The Economic Impacts of Rape*, IFS Working Paper 26/15, 23 February 2026

[Paper PDF](https://ifs.org.uk/sites/default/files/2026-02/WP202615-The-economic-impacts-of-rape.pdf) · [Listing](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ifs/ifsewp/26-15.html)

Abstract, verbatim: "We estimate the economic impacts of rape using population-wide Finnish police reports and hospital admissions. Over the five years following a rape, victims' employment falls by 6 percentage points and annual earnings decline by €1,500, alongside a 6 percentage point increase in anti-depressant use, relative to otherwise observationally equivalent women. These effects persist for at least a decade and correspond to a 15% decline in employment, 17% earnings loss, and 28% rise in anti-depressant use relative to pre-assault baselines. Rape also generates spillover effects to victims' networks: female schoolmates and victims' parents experience significant mental health deterioration."

Young victims, verbatim: "We find that young victims who experienced a rape in adolescence are 10 p.p. less likely to graduate college, 14 p.p. less likely to be employed, and 6 p.p. more likely to be prescribed anti-depressants at age 25 compared to young women with similar pre-rape characteristics. The decline in college completion rates is particularly pronounced amongst the most academically able teenage victims."

Age composition, verbatim: "We find that rape victims are young: 41% of victims are below the age of 21 when they are assaulted."

Peer spillover, verbatim: "We find a 0.2 increase in the number of female classmates taking anti-depressant prescriptions and no effect for male classmates."

*Complexities:* Finland, 2006–2019, using police reports and hospital admissions — a different country, a different legal system, and by construction only assaults that reach an official record. The design is a matched-control event study, not a randomized experiment; the authors describe the estimates as causal under their matching assumptions and support them with a "future victims" placebo design. The €1,500 is in euros, not dollars, and is not converted on the page. The comparison group is "otherwise observationally equivalent women," not the general population.

### Academic outcomes on U.S. campuses

Source: [Molstad, Weinhardt & Jones, *Sexual Assault as a Contributor to Academic Outcomes in University: A Systematic Review*, Trauma, Violence & Abuse (2023)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/15248380211030247). Abstract, verbatim: "We identified 13 articles that examined academic consequences of sexual assault during university. Across all studies, sexual assault was associated with more academic problems including lower grade point average, dropping out of university, and self-regulated learning problems... Although the number of articles is small, the results are consistent."
*Complexities:* These are **associations** from observational studies, not causal estimates. The page quotes the review's own wording rather than paraphrasing it, and attaches no multiplier. Confirmed a second time from the abstract of record via Europe PMC.

### Physical consequences reported in AAU

AAU 2019, p. 27: physical injuries from penetration reported by 15.1% of women, 18.9% of TGQN students and 7.6% of men; sexually transmitted infections reported for 4.6% of women.

## Section 6 — The wider society

Source: [CDC, *About Sexual Violence*, updated 17 December 2025](https://www.cdc.gov/sexual-violence/about/index.html), drawing on NISVS:
- "Nearly half of women and more than 1 in 6 men experienced some form of contact sexual violence in their lifetimes."
- "More than 1 in 5 women and 1 in 31 men have experienced completed or attempted rape in their lifetimes."
- "More than 4 in 5 female rape survivors reported that they were first raped before age 25 and almost half were first raped as a minor (i.e., before age 18)."
- "Estimates put the lifetime cost of rape at $122,461 per survivor, including medical care, lost productivity from work, and criminal justice costs."

Student vs. non-student comparison: BJS NCJ 248471 — "The rate of rape and sexual assault was 1.2 times higher for nonstudents (7.6 per 1,000) than for students (6.1 per 1,000)" — and "Rape and sexual assault victimizations of students (80%) were more likely than nonstudent victimizations (67%) to go unreported to police."

*Complexities:* The CDC lifetime figures and the NCVS annual figures come from different surveys with different definitions and cannot be arithmetically combined. The NCVS student/non-student comparison covers ages 18–24 only.

**Support line printed on the page.** "Call 800.656.HOPE (4673) to be connected with a trained staff member from a sexual assault service provider in your area" — the RAINN National Sexual Assault Hotline, as listed on the same CDC page.

## Recurring caveats

**Prevalence is not incidence.** A prevalence rate answers "what share of women experienced this at
least once?"; an incidence rate answers "how many incidents occurred per 1,000 people?" The two cannot
be compared directly, and the page flags every place where the distinction matters.

**Cumulative is not annual.** "Since enrolling" covers up to four or more years and rises with each
year in school; an academic-year rate covers about nine months. Comparing them without saying so is
the single most common error in this literature.

**Non-response can push either way.** As the AAU report itself states: "if non-respondents are more
likely to be victimized, then there will be a negative bias in the estimates (i.e., the estimated
victimization rate will be too low). If non-respondents are less likely to be victimized, then
estimates are too high." A 21.9% response rate widens that band in both directions; it does not by
itself establish that an estimate is too high.

**Survey categories are not criminal categories.** No survey figure on this page is a count of crimes,
charges or convictions, and none should be read as one.

**Samples are not the country.** AAU's 33 schools are large research universities and skew private;
CCSVS is nine schools chosen for a methodological pilot. Neither is a national sample of American
higher education.
