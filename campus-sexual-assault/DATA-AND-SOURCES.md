# Campus sexual assault: how the numbers are made — data and sources

**Purpose.** This is the companion ledger to `index.html`. Every figure in the story appears here with
the exact source, the supporting language from that source (quoted), and any complexity a careful
reader should weigh. Nothing appears on the page that is not in this file.

**Rule used throughout.** No figure is published unless it was read in the primary document — the
federal report, the journal article, the working paper or the agency table itself. Where a number is
derived (arithmetic performed here rather than published by the source), it is labeled *derived* both
here and on the page.

**Maintainer note.** When a number changes in `index.html`, change it here too.

**Revision, September 16, 2026.** An independent review of every figure and of the methodology found
errors that are now corrected. The main changes:
- The opening axis now compares victimization rates with victimization rates. The earlier version said
  the like-for-like gap was "narrower" than the axis showed and "roughly tenfold." That was backwards:
  for completed rape the gap is 27 times.
- The AAU marker now shows all undergraduate women, not first-year women only.
- The Clery marker is now labeled per 1,000 students of all genders.
- The dial labels now use the AAU's "only" categories, and the dial shows the 2.5 percent "both" group.
- The nonresponse passage now reports what the AAU and the validation study actually found.
- The nine-school chart now has confidence intervals and explains the published average.
- The Clery 40 is shown as a separate comparison, not a stage of the funnel.
- The Finnish classmate effect was overstated fivefold and is now corrected, and the paper's "rape"
  category is described.
- The Fisher, Cullen and Turner comparison study is added.
- The Pew public-opinion section was removed because it had no connection to sexual assault.

## The instruments

| Short name | Full name | Who runs it | Field dates | Mode | Response rate |

| CCSVS | Campus Climate Survey Validation Study, Final Technical Report (NCJ 249545) | Bureau of Justice Statistics + RTI International | Fielded March 2015, nine schools; "data collection periods and contact schedules varied slightly across schools" | Web | Average 54% women (43%–71% by school), 40% men |
| AAU | Report on the AAU Campus Climate Survey on Sexual Assault and Misconduct (2019) | Association of American Universities / Westat | Spring 2019, 33 schools | Web | 21.9% overall; 26.1% women |
| NCWSV | Fisher, Cullen & Turner, *The Sexual Victimization of College Women* (NCJ 182369, 2000) | National Institute of Justice; comparison component funded by BJS | Late March to mid-May 1997, about the 1996–97 academic year | Telephone | 85.6% main sample; 91.6% comparison sample |
| Meta-analysis | Steele et al., *The Prevalence of Sexual Assault Among Higher Education Students: A Systematic Review With Meta-Analyses*, Trauma, Violence & Abuse (2024) | Academic | 131 articles, 21 countries | Varies | Varies |

NCVS method, verbatim from [BJS](https://bjs.ojp.gov/data-collection/ncvs): "eligible persons in these
households are interviewed every 6 months, either in person or over the phone, for a total of seven
interviews"; "Annual NCVS estimates are based on the number and characteristics of crimes respondents
experienced during the prior 6 months, not including the month in which they were interviewed." NCJ
248471, methodology: "Longitudinal surveys like the NCVS have the advantage of bounding the reference
period"; "In 2013, 90,630 households and 160,040 persons age 12 or older were interviewed for the NCVS...
The response rate was 84% for households and 88% for eligible persons."

CCSVS response rates, CCSVS p. ES-5: "The average response rate across all nine schools was 54% for
females and 40% for males." p. ES-5: "Response rates for females ranged from 43% (School 4) to 71% (School 5)."

## Hero

**"6.1 per 1,000" (NCVS annual rate, female students 18–24).**
Source: [BJS, NCJ 248471](https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/rape-and-sexual-assault-among-college-age-females-1995-2013), Sinozich & Langton, table 1 — "The rate of rape and sexual assault was 1.2 times higher for nonstudents (7.6 per 1,000) than for students (6.1 per 1,000)."
*Complexities:* An **incidence** rate (victimizations per 1,000 per year), averaged over 1995–2013. It
includes completed rape (2.0), attempted rape (1.5), sexual assault (1.9) and threat of rape or sexual
assault (0.7) — table 1. The hero label says "including attempts and threats."

**"10.3%" (CCSVS academic-year prevalence, undergraduate women).**
Source: [CCSVS Final Technical Report](https://bjs.ojp.gov/content/pub/pdf/ccsvsftr.pdf), p. ES-6 — "The prevalence rate for completed sexual assault experienced by undergraduate females during the 2014–2015 academic year, averaged across the nine schools, was 10.3%, and ranged from 4.2% at School 2 to 20.0% at School 1."
*Complexities:* Share of women, not incidents. The reference period runs from the start of the academic
year to the survey, fielded in spring 2015 — less than a full year.

**"25.9%" (AAU cumulative prevalence, undergraduate women).**
Source: [AAU 2019 report, p. 14](https://www.aau.edu/sites/default/files/AAU-Files/Key-Issues/Campus-Safety/Revised%20Aggregate%20report%20%20and%20appendices%201-7_(01-16-2020_FINAL).pdf) — "the estimate for women undergraduates is between two and three times higher at 25.9 percent than women graduate and professional students, at 9.7 percent."
*Complexities:* Cumulative — since enrolling at the school. Covers penetration, attempted penetration or
sexual touching by physical force or inability to consent. It is an average of the 33 schools' rates.

CCSVS p. ES-7, verbatim: "The sexual assault victimization incidence rate for completed sexual assault,
averaged across the nine participating schools, was 176 per 1,000 undergraduate females, and ranged from
85 at School 2 to 325 at School 1... The average victimization incidence rate for rape per 1,000
undergraduate females was 54, and ranged from 28 at School 9 to 110 at School 5."

NCJ 248471, table 1, students: total 6.1; completed rape 2.0; attempted rape 1.5; sexual assault 1.9;
threat of rape or sexual assault 0.7.

AAU table A7-5 (undergraduate women), "Current School Year": "Total involving physical force or
inability to consent or stop what was happening 12.8." AAU glossary: "Current school year refers to the
academic year beginning with the Fall 2018 term."

*Derivation, Clery marker:* 5,839 on-campus rapes reported in calendar 2021 ÷ 18.6 million students
(15.4M undergraduate + 3.2M postbaccalaureate, fall 2021) × 1,000 = **0.31 per 1,000 students**.
Clery counts victims of any gender, including non-students, so the denominator is all students.

*Derived ratio:* completed rape, CCSVS 54 ÷ NCVS 2.0 = **27 times**. The CCSVS reference period is shorter
than a year, so an annualized gap would be larger still. Not computed on the page.

*2013 NCVS rate,* NCJ 248471: "In 2013, college-age females had a similar rate of rape and sexual assault
regardless of enrollment status (about 4.3 victimizations per 1,000)."

*Complexities — read before comparing:*
1. **Incidence vs. prevalence.** NCVS and the two CCSVS markers are incidence rates and can be compared
   with each other. The AAU marker is a prevalence rate (share of women) and includes attempts and
   touching; the page says it cannot be compared directly with the rows above it.
2. **The Clery marker is not a victimization rate.** It counts incidents reported under Clery's geography
   and reporting rules.
3. **Different populations.** NCVS is nationally representative of 18–24-year-old female students
   (including trade and vocational students); CCSVS is nine schools; AAU is 33 schools.
4. **Different years.** 1995–2013 (NCVS), 2014–15 (CCSVS), 2018–19 (AAU), 2021 (Clery).

The earlier version plotted the AAU's first-year women (16.1%, AAU p. 16). AAU p. 16: "Among first year
students, 16.1 percent... This percentage steadily declines by year in school to 11.3 percent for fourth
year (or higher) students." Replaced with the all-undergraduate figure.

### The wording experiment (Fisher, Cullen & Turner)

Source: [NCJ 182369 (OJP)](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/nij/182369.pdf); also at [ERIC ED449712](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED449712.pdf).
- Main sample: "a telephone survey of a randomly selected, national sample of 4,446 women who were
  attending a 2- or 4-year college or university during fall 1996."
- Comparison: "The comparison component used the same two-stage methodology as the main study except
  victimization was measured by using the screen questions and the incident report employed by NCVS...
  The sample size was 4,432 college women; the response rate was 91.6 percent."
- Result, p. 21: "The percentage of the sample that reported experiencing a completed rape in the
  comparison study was 11 times smaller than the percentage of victims in the main component (0.16
  percent compared with 1.7 percent)."
- Conclusion: "assuming that the methodology used in this study is defensible, it seems likely that NCVS
  underestimates the true incidence of rape victimization in the United States."

*Complexities:* The authors note one difference from the real NCVS: NCVS estimates are adjusted using
interviewers' verbatim descriptions of incidents, and the comparison component's were not.

### AAU response rate

AAU 2019, p. 5 — "The final response rate was 21.9 percent (Table 2). This rate varied by gender (17.5%
men, 26.1% women)..." School-level response rates ranged "from a low of 6 percent to a high of 68.0 percent."

### AAU's own comparison to CCSVS

AAU 2019, p. 45 — "The CCVS estimate for undergraduate women being a victim of nonconsensual contact since
enrolled (20.5%) is somewhat below the AAU estimate (24.9%)..." AAU abbreviates the study "CCVS"; the 24.9%
AAU figure is the 2015 survey. Not used on the page.

### Meta-analysis pooled estimates

Source: [Steele, Martin, Sciarra, Melendez-Torres, Degli Esposti & Humphreys (2024)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/15248380231196119); full text at [Europe PMC, PMC11155219](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC11155219).
"Of which, 131 articles were included with 113 reporting on women's victimization." Pooled prevalence
for women 17.5% (95% CI 15.3–20.0), from 97 study estimates (Table 4). Rape, women: 5.9%. "All
meta-analyses had considerable heterogeneity (I² > 97%)."

**Recall-period comparison.** Table 4, women, overall:

| Recall period | Pooled prevalence | 95% CI | Estimates |
|---|---|---|---|
| Past year | 0.180 | .148–.216 | 47 |
| Entire time at a higher-education institution | 0.168 | .138–.203 | 50 |
| Overall | 0.175 | .153–.20 | 97 |

*Complexities:* Different, largely non-overlapping sets of studies; the intervals overlap. The page
presents the comparison as a sign of dispersion in the literature. The meta-analysis is international.

## Section 2 — The definitions dial

Six values, all AAU 2019, undergraduate women, since enrolling at the school. They are AAU's own published
subtotals — **not sums computed here** — and they overlap at the person level.

AAU table A7-5, "Since Entering College": Total 25.9; Penetration 12.8; Physical force only 7.3 (completed
4.9, attempted 3.5); Inability to consent or stop what was happening only 5.4; Both physical force and
inability to consent 2.5; Sexual touching 19.6.

| Dial option | Rate | Source language (AAU 2019, p. 15, unless noted) |
|---|---|---|
| Penetration by physical force only | 7.3% | "Among undergraduate women, 7.3 percent reported penetration by physical force"; table A7-5 label "Physical force only" |
| Penetration by inability to consent only | 5.4% | "and 5.4 percent reported penetration related to an inability to consent"; table label "...only" |
| Penetration involving both tactics | 2.5% | "Instances of physical force and inability to consent together were reported by 2.5 percent of undergraduate women" |
| Any penetration, any of these tactics | 12.8% | "undergraduate women had a prevalence rate of 12.8 percent compared to 4.6 percent of women graduate/professional students" |
| Sexual touching | 19.6% | "among women, 19.6 percent of undergraduates reported sexual touching compared to 6.9 percent of graduates/professionals" |
| All contact by force or inability to consent | 25.9% | p. 14; see hero note |

AAU p. 15: "Acts involving penetration or attempted penetration by physical force or inability to consent
meet the legal definitions for rape in many jurisdictions."

*Complexities:*
- Completed and attempted subtotals (4.9 + 3.5 = 8.4) exceed 7.3, and 7.3 + 5.4 + 2.5 = 15.2 exceeds 12.8,
  because one woman can report several incidents.
- The AAU also measured nonconsensual contact by coercion and without voluntary agreement (AAU section
  3.2; glossary pp. v–vi). Those are not in the 25.9 percent, so it is not "all" nonconsensual contact.
- "Inability to consent" is defined by the respondent's own judgment that they were "unable to consent or
  stop what was happening." Survey categories and criminal categories differ.
- The earlier version said 25.9 percent "became '1 in 4.'" That claim was removed. The "1 in 4" phrase
  predates this survey. CCSVS p. 76 notes that an earlier estimate (Krebs et al., 2009) "has been used
  widely to suggest that 1 in 5 undergraduate females will be sexually assaulted while in college."

### Nonresponse

- AAU 2019, p. 7: "Analysis of the 2015 data found some evidence that the non-response is in a positive
  direction—that is the published estimates were too high, although not by a large amount... The analysis
  in Appendix 4 confirms this conclusion."
- AAU 2019, p. xvii: "there is little evidence that non-response bias can explain the high rates of
  victimization found in either the 2015 or 2019 surveys."
- CCSVS p. 50: "for auxiliary student characteristics available for both respondents and nonrespondents,
  there is little evidence of nonresponse bias."
- CCSVS p. ES-5: "students who took the survey relatively early in the field period reported experiencing
  sexual assault victimization at the same rate as those who took the survey later in the field period."

The earlier version said low response "means the band of uncertainty is wider in both directions." That
conflated bias with sampling error and omitted the AAU's own finding. Corrected.

## Section 4 — What reaches the record

### The nine-school funnel

Source: CCSVS, p. 110 — "male and female undergraduate students at the nine institutions participating in
the CCSVS experienced a total of 2,380 incidents of completed rape during the 2014–15 academic year, of
which 770 (32%) occurred on campus. According to the CCSVS data, about 60 of these rape incidents (margin of
error between 30 and 90 incidents) were committed on campus and reported to school authorities. In other
words, approximately 3% of all completed rapes captured by the CCSVS would be expected to be included under
Clery reporting standards. When comparing... with actual Clery data for these institutions based on the most
recent Clery data available (2014 calendar year), the CCSVS estimate (60 rape incidents) was not
statistically different from the Clery number of rapes reported (40 rape incidents)."

BJS's conclusion, same page: "the CCSVS data suggest that the vast majority of rapes are not reported to
authorities and are not represented in an institution's Clery numbers."

*Complexities:* The 40 is a calendar-2014 Clery count covering victims of any status. It is not a subset
of the survey's 60, so the page draws it as a separate comparison marker. BJS caveats: Clery covers all
students and employees while CCSVS covers undergraduates; some victimized students may have left school;
definitions differ. Bar widths (derived): 770 ÷ 2,380 = 32.4%; 60 ÷ 2,380 = 2.5%; 40 ÷ 2,380 = 1.7%.

### Reporting rates and reasons

- CCSVS, p. 107: "Across all nine schools, 4.3% of sexual battery incidents and 12.5% of rape incidents were
  reported by the victim to any official."
- AAU 2019, p. 28: "For incidents involving penetration, women contacted a program for 29.5 percent of
  incidents... The proportion of incidents involving penetration in which the victim contacted a program
  across the 33 schools varies from 16.5 to 65.6 percent."
- AAU 2019, p. 30 and table 21 (per 100 victimizations where a program was not contacted), women,
  penetration, any reason: could handle it themselves 48.8; not serious enough 47.4; embarrassed, ashamed
  or too emotionally difficult 41.7; did not want to get the person in trouble 24.5; did not think
  resources could help 21.9. The page shows these five.
- Most important reason, AAU p. 31: "the most common response following incidents involving penetration
  was that she 'could handle it herself' (20.0%). The next most common responses were that it was not
  serious enough (16.8%)..."
- CCSVS, p. 109: officials were considered helpful for "81% of rape incidents and 79% of sexual battery
  incidents that were reported to any official."

The earlier version said the largest barrier "is a judgment about the seriousness." "Could handle it
myself" ranks first both ways. Corrected.

### National administrative count

Source: [NCES Digest table 329.10](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_329.10.asp) —
reported on-campus rapes, all institutions: 4,431 (2014); 5,119 (2015); 5,840 (2016); 6,583 (2017); 6,682

## Section 5 — What follows for victims

### Adams, Huttunen, Nix & Zhang, *The Economic Impacts of Rape*, IFS Working Paper 26/15, February 2026

[Paper PDF](https://ifs.org.uk/sites/default/files/2026-02/WP202615-The-economic-impacts-of-rape.pdf) · [Listing](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ifs/ifsewp/26-15.html)

Abstract, verbatim: "Over the five years following a rape, victims' employment falls by 6 percentage
points and annual earnings decline by €1,500, alongside a 6 percentage point increase in anti-depressant
use, relative to otherwise observationally equivalent women. These effects persist for at least a decade
and correspond to a 15% decline in employment, 17% earnings loss, and 28% rise in anti-depressant use
relative to pre-assault baselines."

Young victims, pp. 4–5: "we compare the age-25 outcomes of young girls who experience a rape to young girls
with the same (pre-event) GPA and mental health outcomes and who come from observationally similar families
in terms of parents' education and income... young victims who experienced a rape in adolescence are 10 p.p.
less likely to graduate college, 14 p.p. less likely to be employed... The decline in college completion
rates is particularly pronounced amongst the most academically able teenage victims."

Age: "41% of victims are below the age of 21 when they are assaulted."

Peer spillover, p. 5: "We find a 0.2 increase in the number of female classmates taking anti-depressant
prescriptions and no effect for male classmates. This can be interpreted as one additional girl being on
anti-depressants in every five classes in which an adolescent girl is assaulted." The earlier version said
"one additional classmate on medication," five times too large. Corrected.

Case definition, pp. 8–9: "we collectively refer to all of these crime types as 'Rape'... 50% of the cases
are flagged as Rape or Aggravated Rape. 41% of cases are Sexual Abuse, Coercion into a Sexual Act, or Sexual
Harassment. The remaining cases are those where one of the primary crimes was attempted."

*Complexities:* Finland, 2006–2019; a working paper, not yet published in a journal; only assaults that
reached police or hospital records, which may be more severe than many survey-counted incidents; a
matched-control event study with a "future victims" design, not an experiment. The earlier version called
it "the largest recent study," which was not supported. Removed.

### Academic outcomes on campuses

Source: [Molstad, Weinhardt & Jones (2023)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/15248380211030247). Abstract, verbatim: "We identified 13 articles that examined academic consequences of sexual assault during university. Across all studies, sexual assault was associated with more academic problems including lower grade point average, dropping out of university, and self-regulated learning problems... Although the number of articles is small, the results are consistent."
*Complexities:* Associations from observational studies, not causal estimates.

### Physical consequences reported in AAU

AAU 2019, p. 28: "Physical injuries from penetration were reported by 15.1 percent of women." Table 20 is

## Section 6 — Beyond campus

Student vs. non-student, NCJ 248471, table 1: students 6.1, nonstudents 7.6 per 1,000; ratio 1.2. The
nonstudent total carries "‡ Nonstudent estimates are significantly different from student estimates at the
90% confidence level." Completed rape (3.1 vs 2.0) is significant at 95%. Unreported to police: 80% of
student victimizations vs 67% of nonstudent. Students "enrolled part time or full time in a post-secondary
institution"; the report describes them as enrolled in "a college, university, trade school or vocational
school."

Age risk, NCJ 248471 (figure 2): "From 1997 to 2013, females ages 18 to 24 consistently experienced higher
rates of rape and sexual assault than females in other age brackets."

[CDC, *About Sexual Violence*, updated December 17, 2025](https://www.cdc.gov/sexual-violence/about/index.html), drawing on NISVS:
- "Nearly half of women and more than 1 in 6 men experienced some form of contact sexual violence in their lifetimes."
- "More than 1 in 5 women and 1 in 31 men have experienced completed or attempted rape in their lifetimes."
- "More than 4 in 5 female rape survivors reported that they were first raped before age 25 and almost half were first raped as a minor (i.e., before age 18)."
- "Estimates put the lifetime cost of rape at $122,461 per survivor, including medical care, lost productivity from work, and criminal justice costs."

*Complexities:* CDC lifetime figures and NCVS annual figures come from different surveys and cannot be
combined. The student/non-student comparison inherits the NCVS's measurement; the page notes that the
survey cannot show whether students are less likely to describe an incident to an interviewer as a crime.

**Support line printed on the page.** 800.656.HOPE (4673), the RAINN National Sexual Assault Hotline, as
listed on the same CDC page.

### Closing note on measurement direction

The page lists choices that tend to raise published figures (broader definitions, longer recall,
behaviorally specific screening, a sexual-misconduct frame) and choices that tend to lower them (a crime
frame, screening questions that do not describe specific acts, bounded six-month recall). Support: Fisher,
Cullen & Turner (wording); NCJ 248471 methodology (bounding and telescoping); CCSVS p. 76 (recall over 3.5
years).

## Recurring caveats

**Prevalence is not incidence.** A prevalence rate answers "what share of women experienced this at
least once?"; an incidence rate answers "how many incidents occurred per 1,000 people?"

**Cumulative is not annual.** "Since enrolling" covers up to four or more years; an academic-year rate
covers the months from the start of the fall term to the survey.

**Low response raises the risk of bias; it is not the same as sampling error.** The AAU's own analysis
found a small upward bias; the CCSVS found little evidence of bias. See section 2.

**Survey categories are not criminal categories.** No survey figure on this page is a count of crimes,
charges or convictions.

**Samples are not the country.** AAU's 33 schools and the CCSVS's nine schools are not national samples of
American higher education. The AAU: "The variation across schools emphasizes the importance of not
generalizing from these 33 schools to a larger population (e.g. national)."
