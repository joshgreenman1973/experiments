Is the subway safer than the street?

On raw counts, the subway accounts for about 1 in 17 public-space violent crimes. But raw counts are meaningless without a denominator: how much time do people spend in each environment?

How the original analysis framed it

Before extending the analysis, here is the original methodology. The Vital City article asked about exposure, a step most crime coverage skips entirely.

1Count crimes
2Estimate exposure
3Compute rates
4Compare

Step 1: Isolating public-space violence

The article focused on violent index crimes—murders, rapes, robberies, and felony assaults—comparing only those that occurred in the transit system to those on streets and public outdoor spaces. This is the right comparison: it excludes domestic violence, commercial burglaries, and other crimes that occur in contexts where subway-vs.-street framing makes no sense.

Step 2: Estimating subway exposure

The subway side is relatively tractable. In 2023, riders took a little over 1.3 billion trips, counting MTA’s 1.15 billion paid entries plus an allowance for fare evasion. Each trip involves an average of ~30 minutes on a train plus ~10 minutes of platform time, stair-climbing, and tunnel-walking. That yields about 880 million person-hours of subway exposure per year.

A note on “person-hours.” This denominator captures total time everyone collectively spends in the subway system. It doesn’t matter whether those hours come from 5 million daily riders taking two trips each or from 2 million riders taking five trips each. For risk calculation, the question is how many crimes occur for every hour anyone is down there. A frequent rider accumulates more hours and proportionally more exposure—the rate per hour stays the same.

Step 3: The key insight

The article calculated a subway violent crime rate of roughly 1.3 crimes per million person-hours. Then it asked what the street rate would need to be to equal this. New Yorkers would need to collectively spend 12.5+ billion person-hours on the streets each year for the per-hour risk to match the subway’s. Spread across the roughly 9.5 million people in the city on a given day (residents, commuters and tourists), that is more than 25 hours a week each, outdoors on the street.

Step 4: Where this analysis extends it

The Vital City article made a qualitative case, but stopped short of computing a street crime rate. The analysis below takes the next step: building three independent estimates of how many person-hours New Yorkers actually spend on streets, so we can calculate a direct per-hour comparison and let you stress-test the assumptions. It uses the same approach as the article, updated to 2025 crime and ridership data.

The hard part: estimating how much time New Yorkers spend on the street

The subway estimate is relatively tight. The street side carries more uncertainty, and it is where most existing analyses fall short.

The subway in 2025

MTA counted 1.28 billion paid subway entries in 2025. Its quarterly surveys put subway fare evasion at an average of 10.2% that year, which brings total trips to about 1.43 billion. At the same 40 minutes a trip, that is 952 million person-hours. (NYC DOT’s 2024 Mobility Survey, which times each leg of a trip, puts the average subway trip at about 42 minutes in the system, so 40 is a fair round number.)

Three independent estimates of street time

Method 1: Walking-trip decomposition. NYC DOT’s 2024 Citywide Mobility Survey finds adult New Yorkers make 3.67 trips a day, averaged over the whole week, and 36.7% of them are made entirely on foot, averaging 22.2 minutes each. With an effective daily population of ~9.8M (8.6M residents + 1M commuters + 200K tourists), that yields ~13.1M walking trips a day = ~4.9M person-hours/day, or ~1.78B/year from walking trips alone.
Method 2: Time-use approach. The same survey shows adult New Yorkers spend about 39 minutes a day walking as travel: 30 minutes on walk-only trips plus 9 minutes walking to and from transit. Allowing for outdoor time that isn’t a trip, assume 45 min/day for the 8.6M residents, 30 min for 1M commuters on workdays, and 120 min for tourists. That yields ~2.6B person-hours/year. But this includes all outdoor time, not just street-adjacent exposure—discount by ~30% for time in parks, plazas, and private outdoor spaces to get ~1.83B for street-comparable spaces.
Method 3: Residual-time / activity accounting. Of 24 hours, the average New Yorker spends ~8 hrs sleeping, ~8.5 hrs at work/school (indoors), ~4.5 hrs at home awake, ~0.7 hrs on transit. That leaves ~2.3 hrs for “other”—errands, walking, socializing, dining out, etc. Not all is street-exposed; estimate ~60% is, yielding ~1.38 hrs/day × 9.8M = ~4.9B person-hours/year. But much of this is in cars, buildings, or otherwise sheltered. Applying a 30–45% “true street exposure” factor: ~1.48–2.22B.

These are estimates, not predictions. Every number here is a citywide average built from reported crimes and from assumptions about how people spend their time, and no one actually faces the average. Risk varies greatly by neighborhood (the setting below captures some of that, roughly) and also by station and block, time of day, how long you linger, your age and circumstances and many other factors this calculator can’t see. How to read this: “Your Annual Subway Risk” is the probability that you personally would be the victim of a violent index crime in the subway system over the course of a year, given how many hours you spend there. The “ratio” card shows how much more total risk your street time generates compared to your subway time.

Neighborhood adjustment applies to street risk only. The subway is a system-wide network—a Mott Haven resident rides through Midtown on the same train as an Upper East Sider—so transit crime doesn’t vary cleanly by neighborhood. But surface crime rates vary enormously: Mott Haven’s violent crime rate per resident is 3.2× the citywide average, while Bayside’s is about a quarter of it. That’s a 12× gap within the same city. The calculator uses that rate as a stand-in for street risk in each neighborhood.

Six hypotheses for why the gap is so large

Several mechanisms likely contribute to the gap, and they reinforce each other.

1. Surveillance density. The subway is one of the most camera-saturated environments in New York. Platform cameras, in-car cameras, and uniformed officers at turnstiles create a deterrence environment that open streets cannot match, and offenders know it.
2. Channelized movement. Subway riders flow through controlled chokepoints—turnstiles, platform edges, car doors. Offenders have fewer escape routes and victims have more bystanders nearby. Streets offer infinite approach and escape vectors.
3. Population composition effects. Subway riders skew toward commuters—employed, sober, purposeful travelers. Street populations include all of these plus people in crisis, intoxicated individuals, and those involved in street-level disputes. The “interaction pool” on streets includes more potential offenders and more high-risk encounters.
4. Temporal concentration. Subway ridership concentrates heavily in rush hours. Streets have 24/7 exposure, including the late-night hours when a large share of street violence occurs. The subway’s time-of-day profile may steer much of its exposure away from the highest-risk hours.
5. Dispute geography. Most violent crime arises from disputes, robberies, and drug markets—activities overwhelmingly situated on the surface. The subway is a transit conduit, not a place where people live, deal, argue, or drink. It’s inherently less “criminogenic” as an environment.
6. Geographic uniformity as a safety feature. Above ground, crime risk is hyper-localized: a block in Brownsville can be 20× more dangerous than a block in Park Slope. Savvy New Yorkers navigate this geography instinctively—avoiding certain blocks, crossing the street, staying in well-lit areas. But the subway flattens geography. When you swipe in at a station, you enter a single connected system. A rider on the A train passes through Midtown and East New York in the same trip. You can’t “avoid the bad neighborhoods” underground the way you can on foot—but you don’t need to, because the system’s overall risk is so much lower. Subway violence is not perfectly even: in 2025, half of violent subway crimes were reported at 56 station names, out of 472 stations (NYPD’s data merges some same-named stations, such as the several at 14th Street). It is still far more spread out than street violence, so the subway’s average rate is closer to its typical rate—unlike the street, where the average conceals enormous local variation and the worst blocks drive much of the total. This analysis does not measure per-hour rates at individual stations.

What this model cannot tell you

Reporting bias. Subway crime may be reported at higher rates (more cameras, more witnesses, more police presence) than street crime, which would make the true gap even larger than measured.

Crime type matters. The subway has a different crime mix. In 2025, robberies were 40% of its violent index crimes, against 47% on streets and other public spaces, and felony assaults a larger share. A model broken down by crime type would yield different ratios for different offenses.

Seasonality. The walking inputs come from a survey taken in May and June, when New Yorkers walk more than in winter. If anything, that inflates street hours and makes the street look safer than it is.

Geography and time. Both rates vary enormously by station/neighborhood and time of day. A per-hour comparison at 2 AM in East New York vs. Grand Central at rush hour would look very different from these system-wide averages.

Perception vs. reality. The enclosed, inescapable nature of subway cars may make people feel less safe even when they are statistically safer. Fear of crime and actual crime risk are related but distinct phenomena.

Exposure measurement. The street person-hour estimate is the weakest link in this analysis. It implies the average person in the city spends about 30 minutes a day on the street. The original Vital City article sketched a typical working New Yorker’s week at around 18 hours outside, which would put the ratio far lower. Better data—perhaps from MIT’s 2026 Nature Cities model of foot traffic across the city—could narrow the uncertainty range considerably.

Bottom line

6–11×
Per hour spent, the street is six to eleven times more dangerous than the subway, depending on crime category, at the central exposure estimate

The popular fear of subway crime is understandable—an enclosed underground train feels dangerous. But the data are consistent across every crime category: hour for hour, New Yorkers face dramatically higher crime risk on the surface than underground. For violent index crimes, the ratio is ~8×. Include misdemeanor assault and it’s ~6.5× (the subway generates proportionally more low-level altercations). For all index crimes, it’s about 11×. Stretching the street-time estimate to 3 billion hours still leaves the street about 5× riskier per hour for violent crime.

This isn’t an argument that subway crime doesn’t matter. Every violent crime matters. But if the goal is reducing New Yorkers’ overall exposure to violence, the surface—where 94% of public-space violence occurs—is where the largest gains are to be found.

Show full math walkthrough — every calculation, step by step

1. Subway exposure (person-hours)

MTA paid subway entries (2025) = 1,282,466,281 Fare evasion, mean of four quarterly MTA estimates: (9.8% + 9.9% + 11.0% + 10.1%) ÷ 4 = 10.20% All trips: 1,282,466,281 ÷ (1 − 0.102) = 1,428,136,170 Average time in the system per trip (on the train + platforms and stairs) = 40 minutes 1,428,136,170 × 40 ÷ 60 = 952,090,780 person-hours ≈ 952 million   Check against the original (2023): 1,150,217,108 ÷ (1 − 0.12675) × 40 ÷ 60 = 878,112,116 ≈ 880 million The 2023 check reproduces Vital City’s 880 million, which confirms how the article counted fare evaders. NYC DOT’s 2024 Mobility Survey measures about 42 minutes in the subway per trip, so 40 minutes is a fair round number. At 33 minutes, subway hours would fall to about 785 million and the violent-crime ratio to about 6.9×.

2. Street exposure: Method 1 (walking-trip decomposition)

Effective daily population = 8,585,000 residents + 1,000,000 commuters + 200,000 tourists = 9,785,000 Trips per adult per day, averaged over all seven days (2024 Mobility Survey) = 3.666 Share of those trips made entirely on foot = 36.65% Walking trips/day: 9,785,000 × 3.666 × 0.3665 = 13,147,662 Mean walk-only trip = 22.25 minutes Daily person-hours: 13,147,662 × 22.25 ÷ 60 = 4,874,667 hrs/day Annual: 4,874,667 × 365 = 1,779,253,500 ≈ 1.78 billion This is a floor: it leaves out walking to and from transit (another 9 minutes a day per adult in the same survey), standing outside, outdoor dining and waiting at bus stops. The survey covers adult residents only; this method applies their rates to commuters, tourists and children. The median walk-only trip is 9 minutes; the mean is pulled up by long recreational walks, and the mean is what a total requires.

3. Street exposure: Method 2 (time-use approach)

Measured walking as travel, adults (2024 Mobility Survey): 29.9 min walk-only trips + 9.0 min to and from transit = 38.9 min/day Assumed total outdoor pedestrian time, including time that isn’t a trip: 45 min/day Residents (8,585,000 × 45 min/day): 8,585,000 × 45 ÷ 60 × 365 = 2,350,143,750 hrs Commuters (1,000,000 × 30 min/day, 250 workdays): 1,000,000 × 30 ÷ 60 × 250 = 125,000,000 hrs Tourists (200,000 × 120 min/day): 200,000 × 120 ÷ 60 × 365 = 146,000,000 hrs Total outdoor pedestrian time: 2,621,143,750 hrs Discount 30% for parks, plazas, private outdoor spaces: 2,621,143,750 × 0.70 = 1,834,800,625 ≈ 1.83 billion The 45-minute figure and the 30% discount are judgment calls. NYC has extensive park systems (Central Park alone sees about 42M visits a year) and outdoor dining expanded after 2020. These are “outdoor” but not street-comparable for crime exposure purposes.

4. Street exposure: Method 3 (residual-time accounting)

24-hour time budget per person:   Sleep: 8.0 hrs   Work/school (indoors): 8.5 hrs   Home awake: 4.5 hrs   Transit: 0.7 hrs   Remaining: 24 − 8.0 − 8.5 − 4.5 − 0.7 = 2.3 hrs Of remaining, ~60% is street-exposed: 2.3 × 0.60 = 1.38 hrs/day Citywide: 9,785,000 × 1.38 × 365 = 4,928,704,500 hrs Apply 30–45% “true street exposure” factor (much time is in cars, stores, etc.):   Low end: 4,928,704,500 × 0.30 = 1,478,611,350   High end: 4,928,704,500 × 0.45 = 2,217,917,025 Range: 1.48–2.22 billion; midpoint 1,848,264,188 This method has the widest uncertainty because the “true street exposure” factor is hard to pin down empirically. It serves mainly as a sanity check on the other two methods. Its time budget treats every day as a workday, which understates free time for children, retirees and weekends.

5. Triangulated central estimate

Method 1 (walk-only trips): 1,779,253,500 ← likely undercount Method 2 (time-use, adjusted): 1,834,800,625 Method 3 (residual time, midpoint): 1,848,264,188 Average: (1,779,253,500 + 1,834,800,625 + 1,848,264,188) ÷ 3 = 1,820,772,771 Rounded for the model: 1,820 million person-hours/year The three methods span 1.48 to 2.22 billion.

6. Per-million-person-hour crime rates (2025)

Violent index crimes:   Subway: 1,007 ÷ 952 = 1.0578 per million person-hours   Street: 16,003 ÷ 1,820 = 8.7929 per million person-hours   Ratio: 8.7929 ÷ 1.0578 = 8.31×   Violent + misdemeanor assault:   Subway: 2,727 ÷ 952 = 2.8645 per million person-hours   Street: 33,952 ÷ 1,820 = 18.6549 per million person-hours   Ratio: 18.6549 ÷ 2.8645 = 6.51×   All index crimes:   Subway: 2,166 ÷ 952 = 2.2752 per million person-hours   Street: 46,180 ÷ 1,820 = 25.3736 per million person-hours   Ratio: 25.3736 ÷ 2.2752 = 11.15× Equivalent minutes: 60 ÷ 8.31 = 7.2 minutes on the street carry the same violent-crime risk as one hour on the subway.

8. Personal risk calculator formula

The calculator uses a Bernoulli trials model:   Annual risk = 1 − (1 − r)h   Where:   r = crime rate per person-hour = crimes ÷ (exposure in hours)   h = your annual hours = weekly hours × 52   Subway example (5 hrs/week, violent crimes):   r = 1,007 ÷ 952,000,000 = 1.0578e-06 per hour   h = 5 × 52 = 260 hours/year   Risk = 1 − (1 − 1.0578e-06)260 = 1 − 0.999725 = 0.0275% ≈ 1 in 3,637   Street example (10 hrs/week, violent crimes, citywide avg.):   r = 16,003 ÷ 1,820,000,000 = 8.7929e-06 per hour   h = 10 × 52 = 520 hours/year   Risk = 1 − (1 − 8.7929e-06)520 = 1 − 0.995438 = 0.4562% ≈ 1 in 219   With neighborhood multiplier (Mott Haven, 3.20×; the calculator rounds to 3.20):   r = 8.7929e-06 × 3.204 = 2.8172e-05 per hour   Risk = 1 − (1 − 2.8172e-05)520 = 1 − 0.985457 = 1.454% ≈ 1 in 69 The model treats each hour as an independent trial with the same small chance of being victimized. At these rates the result is almost exactly r × h. It counts crimes, not distinct victims, so it slightly overstates the chance of being victimized at least once if some people are victimized more than once.

9. Neighborhood multiplier derivation

Citywide violent crime rate (2025): 47,291 ÷ 8,804,081 = 5.37 per 1,000 residents   Example: Mott Haven (40th Precinct)   Precinct violent crimes: 1,737   Precinct population (2020 Census): 100,929   Precinct rate: 1,737 ÷ 100,929 = 17.21 per 1,000   Multiplier: 17.21 ÷ 5.37 = 3.20×   Example: Bayside (111th Precinct)   Precinct violent crimes: 179   Precinct population (2020 Census): 122,211   Precinct rate: 179 ÷ 122,211 = 1.46 per 1,000   Multiplier: 1.46 ÷ 5.37 = 0.27×   Ratio of highest to lowest neighborhood: 3.204 ÷ 0.273 = 11.8× Multi-precinct neighborhoods add their crimes and residents before dividing. The multiplier counts all violent crime in a precinct, indoors and out, and applies only to street risk. Per-capita rates use residential population as the denominator, which inflates rates in areas with large non-resident populations (Midtown, Lower Manhattan, Downtown Brooklyn).

10. Sensitivity range derivation

Holding subway exposure at 952 million person-hours (1.0578 violent crimes per million):   If street hours = 1.00B: 16,003 ÷ 1,000 = 16.00 → 16.00 ÷ 1.0578 = 15.1× If street hours = 1.20B: 16,003 ÷ 1,200 = 13.34 → 13.34 ÷ 1.0578 = 12.6× If street hours = 1.50B: 16,003 ÷ 1,500 = 10.67 → 10.67 ÷ 1.0578 = 10.1× If street hours = 1.82B: 16,003 ÷ 1,820 = 8.79 → 8.79 ÷ 1.0578 = 8.3× If street hours = 2.20B: 16,003 ÷ 2,200 = 7.27 → 7.27 ÷ 1.0578 = 6.9× If street hours = 3.00B: 16,003 ÷ 3,000 = 5.33 → 5.33 ÷ 1.0578 = 5.0×   Range the methods produce (1.48B–2.22B): 6.8× to 10.2× To bring the ratio to 1:1 you would need about 15.1 billion street person-hours: 29.7 hours a week for each of the 9.79 million people in the city on a given day.
