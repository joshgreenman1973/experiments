How the comparisons are built

The page tracks prices only through series that measure the same thing the same way every month, and compares each one over the same calendar months in two years.

What the page measures, and what it does not

It measures price change: how much more the same goods and services cost now than they did in December 2025, and than they did a year ago. It is not a cost-of-living index for any particular household, and it does not say whether New Yorkers can afford their lives. A household's own inflation depends on what it buys; a renter with a car and a long commute had a different 2026 from a NYCHA tenant who takes the subway.

Where an official price index exists for a concept, the page carries it rather than building its own. Every series is pulled by a script from its publisher; the only hand-entered data are city- and state-set prices (fares, tolls, water rates, the minimum wage and the Rent Guidelines Board orders), each tied to a quoted line on the agency's own page.

The four comparison rules

  1. Same thing, same way. A series qualifies only if a change in it is a change in price, not in what is being priced. BLS indexes qualify because they follow a fixed sample and adjust for quality. Administered prices qualify because a subway ride is the same ride. Medians of whatever happened to be listed or sold in a month do not qualify, because their composition changes; that rule excludes most real-estate medians.
  2. Same months in both years. No BLS series for the New York area is seasonally adjusted. Rather than adjust them ourselves, every "since December" change is shown beside the change over the same months a year earlier (December 2024 to the same month of 2025). Seasonal patterns that recur, such as summer gasoline or the spring apparel season, appear in both and cancel in the comparison. The twelve-month change is shown as well.
  3. Each series at its own latest month, labeled. Every comparison uses the series' own reference month, never the date it was downloaded, and any series whose latest month is earlier than the BLS month says so on the page. Nothing is nowcast, interpolated or carried forward.
  4. Nothing is subtracted across sources. Pay and prices are shown side by side. The page computes no "real wage," because the earnings series covers private jobs located in New York City and the price index covers consumers in a 25-county metro area.

Why December is the base

Each December, BLS publishes the relative importance of every item in each area's index: its share of the index's value at that moment. The New York-area CPI-U is a fixed-basket index within each calendar year, so the December relative importances are exactly the weights that make component changes add up to the all-items change from December to any later month of the next year:

all-items change, Dec. to month t  =  Σi RIi, Dec × (Ii,t / Ii,Dec − 1)

The page uses the eight major groups, which are exhaustive and sum to 100. For December 2025 to September 2026 the eight contributions sum to 3.96 percentage points, and the published all-items change is 3.96%. For December 2024 to September 2025, using the December 2024 relative importances, they sum to 2.80 and the published change is 2.80%. The build script refuses to publish if the two ever differ by more than 0.02 points.

Inside housing and transportation the page shows the components BLS publishes for New York (rent, owners' equivalent rent, furnishings, gasoline) and a remainder computed as the group's contribution minus theirs. The housing remainder carries $7.16 of every $100: household fuels and utilities ($4.20, which BLS no longer publishes separately for New York) plus hotels, second homes and renters' insurance. A December base also matches the question the page asks: what has happened since the year began.

Weights: BLS, Relative importance of components in the Consumer Price Indexes, Table 3, December 2024 (2023 weights) and December 2025 (2024 weights). The December 2025 table is the one BLS uses for the 2026 index.

Whose spending the weights describe

BLS weights the New York-area index by what households across the whole 25-county metro area spend, and that area drives far more than the city does. In the Census Bureau's 2024 American Community Survey, 57 percent of New York City households (1.92 million of 3.38 million) had no vehicle, against 31 percent across the metro area (table B08201). So the area's weights give car costs more importance than they have for most city households.

The page handles this two ways, both exact rather than estimated:

  • Transportation is split into gasoline (a published index), other car costs (private transportation minus gasoline) and public transportation (transportation minus private transportation). The last two are differences of published BLS indexes and weights, so they are exact. Public transportation covers airfares and intercity fares as well as subway, bus and commuter rail; BLS does not publish the split for New York. Nationally, airfares are about 60 percent of the category's weight.
  • All items without car costs is the same fixed-basket index with private transportation removed and the remaining weights rescaled to 100: (all-items change in points minus private transportation's contribution) divided by (100 minus private transportation's weight). It is the price change for the area's basket minus cars, not a survey of what car-free households buy. They also spend differently on everything else, which this does not capture.

The same caution applies to housing. Owners' equivalent rent, an estimate of what owned homes would rent for, carries $26.80 of every $100 in the metro index. Most city households rent (2.32 million renter households in the 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey), and for them the rent index is the one that matters. The page shows both separately for that reason.

Rent

The headline: BLS rent of primary residence

BLS's rent index follows a fixed sample of occupied rental units through its Housing Survey. Each unit is priced every six months; the monthly change in the index is the sixth root of the six-month change in rent across that month's panel. The rent recorded is the full contract rent, "plus any subsidy payment paid to the landlord," so a Section 8 household's rent counts at what the landlord receives, not the tenant's share. Rent-controlled and rent-stabilized apartments are in the sample like any other private rental. Public housing is screened out by design: the BLS Handbook lists "the unit is not public housing" among the eligibility tests.

That makes it the measure of what sitting tenants in private housing pay, including the stabilized million. Its limits: it covers the whole New York-Newark-Jersey City area (the five boroughs, Long Island, the lower Hudson Valley, 12 New Jersey counties and Pike County, Pa.), and BLS does not publish New York City's share of it; the six-month panel smooths and delays turning points by several months; and it says nothing about NYCHA.

Rent-stabilized renewals

The Rent Guidelines Board sets the most a landlord may raise a stabilized rent on renewal. Order 57 allowed 3% on one-year leases and 4.5% on two-year leases starting Oct. 1, 2025, through Sept. 30, 2026. Order 58, adopted 7 to 1 in June 2026, sets 0% for both, for leases starting Oct. 1, 2026, through Sept. 30, 2027. These are ceilings for renewing leases, and tenants feel them only when their lease turns over; a freeze adopted in June reaches all one-year-lease tenants over the following year and two-year-lease tenants over two.

New-lease asking rents

No government agency publishes a monthly or quarterly measure of rents on new leases in New York City. The page uses the Zillow Observed Rent Index for New York city, a private series that compares asking rents on the same listed units over time and is smoothed by Zillow. It is labeled as private and as asking rent everywhere it appears. StreetEasy's median asking rents are not used: a median of whatever is listed shifts with the mix of apartments on the market, and the site blocks automated access, which left the earlier version of this tracker months stale.

Why there is no blended rent figure

The obvious move is to weight stabilized, market and public housing rent changes by their shares of the stock. The page does not do it. The BLS index already is a stock-weighted measure for private rentals, built from a probability sample. A homemade blend would require knowing what share of market-rate tenants renew each month and at what increase, which no public source reports. The page shows the 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey composition (996,600 stabilized, 1,139,000 market-rate, 178,700 NYCHA, 52,810 other regulated and 24,020 rent-controlled apartments, with median contract rents) as context for reading the three measures, not as weights.

Annual benchmark: Census ACS 1-year median contract rent for New York city (table B25058) was $1,624 in 2023 and $1,695 in 2024. The 2025 figure is scheduled for Oct. 29, 2026, and will be added then. ACS medians are not constant-quality, so they are a level check, not a price index.

Energy

BLS stopped publishing New York-area average prices for electricity and piped gas after December 2024, and the New York CPI no longer carries electricity or utility gas sub-indexes. The replacements:

  • Gasoline: EIA's weekly retail price for New York City, regular grade, averaged by calendar month. The latest week is compared with the same week (52 weeks earlier) a year before. BLS's New York-area average gasoline price (APUS12A74714) tracks it closely and is in the data file.
  • Electricity: Con Edison's residential revenue divided by residential kilowatt-hours, from EIA Form 861M. EIA withholds delivery-only (third-party supplier) customers from the monthly file, so this covers households buying both delivery and supply from Con Edison. It is a unit value, not a tariff: it moves with consumption, billing-cycle timing and prior-period adjustments, which is why only same-month comparisons are shown. The data run about two and a half months behind and are marked preliminary. Con Edison serves most of New York City and Westchester.
  • Electricity and piped gas, regional: BLS CPI for the Northeast region (New England, New York, New Jersey and Pennsylvania). Timely and constant-quality, but not New York City.
  • Heating oil: EIA's weekly New York State residential survey, which runs October through March only. Compared week to week a year apart.

Rebuilding a fixed-usage Con Edison bill from the tariff each month was considered and rejected: delivery rates sit in tariff leaves and several monthly adjustment statements, all PDFs, and a reconstruction error would look exactly like a price change.

Pay

Average hourly earnings for private jobs located in New York City, from the BLS payroll survey. The comparison is the latest three months against the same three months a year earlier, because single-city monthly values move by a percent or more from sampling alone. Average hourly earnings change when the mix of jobs changes, not only when pay does; a wave of low-wage hiring lowers the average without lowering anyone's wage. That is the main reason pay is never subtracted from prices on the page.

Left out on purpose

  • Medians of listings and sales (StreetEasy, brokerage reports): their mix changes month to month.
  • Hand surveys of slices, bagels and coffee: a few chosen shops are not a sample of anything, and none were being collected.
  • Broadway average ticket price: it moves with which shows are running, not with what a given seat costs.
  • Property tax rates: a rate is not a bill. What owners pay depends on assessments, which change on their own schedule.
  • Health insurance premiums: the Department of Financial Services approved a 6.0% average for 2027 individual-market plans, effective January. Its 2026 announcement gives cuts from requested rates but not the approved average, so there is no confirmed 2025-to-2026 figure to show. BLS medical care for the New York area is on the page.
  • Taxi fares: TLC's metered rates have not changed since the 2022 rule; the only change in the period is the 75-cent congestion toll added Jan. 5, 2025.
  • Case-Shiller: a private index; FHFA's covers the same ground from a government source.
  • Child care prices for New York City: no monthly or quarterly government series exists. BLS's index for tuition, other school fees and child care in the New York area is on the page.

Known gaps in the data

  • BLS published no October 2025 index during the federal shutdown, and collected November 2025 prices only in the second half of that month. Twelve-month changes for October 2026 will not exist; those for November 2026 will rest on a partial month.
  • The Northeast average price of white bread ends in April 2026 and is no longer shown.
  • EIA 861M data for 2025 and 2026 are preliminary. Con Edison's full-service customer count jumped by about 100,000 between November and December 2025, which may reflect customers returning from third-party suppliers.
  • The FY2027 water bill dollar figure is DEP's proposal times the 6.0% the Water Board adopted.

The pay and rent burden page

The companion page asks whether New Yorkers' incomes keep up, using the same rules: each figure compared only with the same measure over the same span, pay and prices shown side by side and never subtracted.

  • Monthly pay: BLS Current Employment Statistics, average hourly earnings for private-sector jobs located in New York City (SMU36935610500000003), compared over twelve months using three-month averages to damp sampling noise. It is the page's only monthly pay series. An average moves with the mix of jobs as well as with pay.
  • The typical worker: BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, May estimates of the median and average hourly wage for all occupations in the New York-Newark-Jersey City metro area. That area matches the CPI area, so each wage change sits beside the CPI change for the same May-to-May span. This is a deliberate exception to using one earnings series: it is yearly, shown in its own section, and is the only government source of a median wage for the area. BLS notes that these estimates are not designed for comparisons over time, so the page reads them over several years.
  • Rent burden, yearly: Census Bureau American Community Survey 1-year estimates for New York City. The median is B25071, median gross rent as a percentage of household income; the shares come from B25070, with households for which no ratio can be computed left out of the denominator. Gross rent includes utilities. There are no standard 1-year estimates for 2020.
  • Rent burden by income, every three years: the 2023 New York City Housing and Vacancy Survey's median rent-to-income ratio, overall and for renters below the median renter income. Its definitions differ from the ACS, so the two surveys are never compared with each other.
  • Reference measures: the NYCgov Poverty Measure, the Poverty Tracker, Overlooked and Undercounted, the True Cost of Economic Security and the Unheard Third, each quoted from its publisher's latest edition and not recalculated.

Left out on purpose: what a household moving today would pay as a share of income. The only monthly new-lease rent is a private asking-rent index, and dividing it by Census income from a different year would mix sources, years and definitions.

Updating

A script refreshes everything on a schedule: BLS (New York CPI around mid-month), EIA (weekly and monthly), FHFA (quarterly) and Zillow (monthly). City-set prices are rechecked against their sources on each run; a step is added only when the agency's own page confirms a change. If a source fails, the script stops rather than overwriting good data, and the page keeps showing the last month it has, labeled with that month.
