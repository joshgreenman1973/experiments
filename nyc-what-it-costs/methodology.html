How the comparisons are built

Every series on these pages prices the same thing the same way each month, and every change is compared with the same calendar months a year earlier.

Every number on the pages and in this note links to its source. A dotted underline marks the link; hover for the source's name. Figures the pages calculate link to the section below that shows the calculation.

Scope

The price page measures how much the same goods and services cost compared with December 2025 and with a year earlier. A household's own inflation depends on what it buys: a renter with a car and a long commute had a different 2026 from a public housing tenant who takes the subway. The pay page sets earnings and rent burden beside prices.

Where an official price index exists, the pages use it. Scripts pull every series from its publisher. The city- and state-set prices (fares, tolls, water rates, the minimum wage and the Rent Guidelines Board orders) are entered by hand, each tied to a quoted line on the agency's own page.

Four rules

  1. Same thing, same way. A change in the series has to be a change in price. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) indexes follow a fixed sample and adjust for quality. A fare or toll is the same product at every date. Medians of whatever was listed or sold in a month change with the mix of what was listed or sold, so they are left out.
  2. Same months in both years. BLS publishes no seasonally adjusted series for the New York area. Each change from December is shown beside the change over the same months a year earlier, so recurring seasonal swings, like summer gasoline, appear in both. The twelve-month change is shown too.
  3. Each series at its own latest month, labeled. Every change names the months it covers. Nothing is estimated ahead of its release, interpolated or carried forward.
  4. Side by side, never subtracted. The pay series covers private jobs located in New York City; the price index covers consumers in a 25-county metro area. The pages compute no inflation-adjusted wage of their own.

Why December is the base

Each December, BLS publishes the relative importance of every item in each area's index, its share of the index at that moment. The New York-area index uses a fixed basket within each calendar year, so those December shares are the weights that make the parts add up to the whole from December to any later month of the next year:

all-items change, Dec. to month t  =  Σi RIi, Dec × (Ii,t / Ii,Dec − 1)

The pages use the eight major groups, whose weights sum to 100. With data through September 2026, the eight contributions for December 2025 to September 2026 sum to 3.96 points and the published all-items change is 3.96%. For December 2024 to September 2025, using the December 2024 weights, both are 2.80. The build stops if the two ever differ by more than 0.02 points.

Inside housing and transportation, the table shows the parts BLS publishes for New York and a remainder: the group's contribution minus theirs. In the December 2025 weights, the housing remainder carries $7.16 of every $100. Of that, $4.20 is household fuels and utilities, which BLS no longer publishes separately for New York; the rest is hotels, second homes and renters' insurance. Both figures are differences of published weights.

Whose spending the weights describe

BLS weights the index by what households across the whole metro area spend. In the Census Bureau's 2024 American Community Survey, 1.92 million of 3.38 million New York City households, 57 percent, had no vehicle; across the metro area the share was 31 percent. The metro weights give car costs more importance than they have for most city households. The price page handles that two ways, both calculated exactly from published figures:

  • Transportation is split into gasoline (a published index), other car costs (private transportation minus gasoline) and fares (transportation minus private transportation). The fares category covers airlines and intercity travel along with subway, bus and commuter rail. BLS does not publish that split for New York; in the national December 2025 weights, airfares are 0.881 of public transportation's 1.485, or 59 percent.
  • All items without car costs removes private transportation from the same basket and rescales the other weights to 100: the all-items change in points, minus private transportation's contribution, divided by 100 minus private transportation's weight. It describes the metro basket without cars. Households without cars also spend differently on everything else, which this leaves out.

Housing has the same issue. Owners' equivalent rent, BLS's estimate of what owned homes would rent for, carries $26.80 of every $100 in the metro index. The city had 2.32 million renter households in 2023, and the rent index is the one that applies to them. The page shows both.

Rent

BLS rent of primary residence

BLS follows a fixed sample of occupied rental units. Each unit is priced every six months, and the monthly change in the index is the sixth root of the six-month change across that month's panel (BLS Handbook, calculation). The rent recorded is the full contract rent, including any subsidy paid to the landlord, so a Section 8 household counts at what the landlord receives. Rent-controlled and rent-stabilized apartments are in the sample; public housing is screened out (BLS Handbook, design). The index covers the five boroughs, Long Island, the lower Hudson Valley, 12 New Jersey counties and Pike County, Pa. (BLS release, technical note); BLS does not publish New York City's share of it. The six-month panel smooths and delays turning points.

Rent-stabilized renewals

The Rent Guidelines Board sets the most a landlord may raise a stabilized rent on renewal. Order 57 allowed 3% on one-year leases and 4.5% on two-year leases starting Oct. 1, 2025, through Sept. 30, 2026. Order 58, adopted 7 to 1 in June 2026, sets 0% for both, for leases starting Oct. 1, 2026, through Sept. 30, 2027. Tenants feel a guideline when their lease renews, so a freeze adopted in June reaches one-year-lease tenants over the following year and two-year-lease tenants over two.

New-lease asking rents

No government agency publishes a monthly or quarterly new-lease rent for New York City. The price page uses the Zillow Observed Rent Index for New York city, a private series that compares asking rents on the same listed units over time; Zillow smooths it. StreetEasy's median asking rents are left out: a median of what is listed shifts with the mix of apartments on the market, and the site blocks automated access.

No blended rent figure

Weighting stabilized, market and public housing rent changes by their shares of the stock would need the share of market-rate tenants who renew each month and their increases, and no public source reports either. The BLS index is already a sample-based measure of private rentals. The 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey counts of apartments by regulation appear on the page as context.

For a level, the Census Bureau's median contract rent for New York city (table B25058) was $1,624 in 2023 and $1,695 in 2024; the 2025 figure is due Oct. 29, 2026. Medians are not adjusted for quality, so they are not a price index.

Energy

BLS stopped publishing New York-area average prices and indexes for electricity and piped gas after December 2024. The replacements:

  • Gasoline: the Energy Information Administration's (EIA) weekly retail price for New York City, regular grade, averaged by calendar month. The latest week is compared with the week 52 weeks earlier.
  • Con Edison electricity: residential revenue divided by residential kilowatt-hours, from EIA Form 861M. EIA withholds delivery-only customers from the monthly file, so the figure covers households buying both delivery and supply from Con Edison. It moves with usage, billing timing and adjustments as well as rates, so the page compares only the same month a year apart. The data run about 10 weeks behind and are preliminary.
  • Electricity and piped gas, regional: BLS indexes for the Northeast region: New England, New York, New Jersey and Pennsylvania.
  • Heating oil: EIA's weekly New York State residential survey, October through March, compared with the same week a year earlier.

A fixed-usage Con Edison bill rebuilt from the tariff each month was considered and dropped. Delivery charges sit in tariff leaves and several monthly adjustment statements, all PDFs, and a rebuilding error would look like a price change.

Pay and rent burden

  • Monthly pay: BLS average hourly earnings for private-sector jobs located in New York City (SMU36935610500000003). The pages compare three-month averages with the same three months a year earlier, because single months for one city are noisy. An average moves with the mix of jobs as well as with pay: losing low-wage jobs raises it without raising anyone's wage.
  • Median and average wages: BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, May estimates for all occupations in the New York-Newark-Jersey City metro area, the same area as the price index, so each wage change sits beside the price change for the same May-to-May span. These yearly figures are an exception to using one earnings series, because they are the only government source of a median wage for the area. BLS says the estimation method "limits the usefulness of OEWS data for time series analysis," so the page reads them over several years.
  • Rent burden, yearly: American Community Survey 1-year estimates for New York City. The median share is table B25071; the shares paying 30 and 50 percent or more come from B25070, leaving out households for which no ratio can be computed. Rent here includes utilities. The Census Bureau released no standard 1-year estimates for 2020.
  • Rent burden by income: the 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey's median rent-to-income ratios. That survey defines rent and income differently from the American Community Survey, so the two are never compared.
  • Income from all sources: the New York City Comptroller's analysis of state income tax records for 2019 to 2024, quoted with its own inflation adjustment and not recalculated.
  • Other measures of hardship: the NYCgov Poverty Measure, the Poverty Tracker, Overlooked and Undercounted, the True Cost of Economic Security and the Unheard Third, each quoted from its publisher's latest edition.

What a household moving today would pay as a share of income is left out. The only monthly new-lease rent is a private asking-rent index, and dividing it by Census income from another year would mix sources, years and definitions.

Left out on purpose

  • Medians of listings and sales (StreetEasy, brokerage reports): their mix changes month to month.
  • Hand surveys of slices, bagels and coffee: a few chosen shops are no sample of the city, and none were being collected.
  • Broadway average ticket price: it moves with which shows are running.
  • Property tax rates: what owners pay depends on assessments, which change on their own schedule.
  • Health insurance premiums: the Department of Financial Services approved a 6.0% average increase for 2027 individual-market plans. Its 2026 announcement gave cuts from requested rates and no approved average, so there is no confirmed figure for 2025 to 2026. BLS medical care for the New York area is on the price page.
  • Taxi fares: the Taxi and Limousine Commission's metered rates date from its 2022 rule; the 75-cent congestion toll on trips (TLC) began with congestion pricing on Jan. 5, 2025.
  • Case-Shiller: a private index; the Federal Housing Finance Agency's covers the same ground.
  • Child care prices for New York City: no monthly or quarterly government series exists. BLS's New York-area index for tuition, other school fees and child care is on the price page.

Known gaps in the data

  • BLS published no October 2025 index during the federal shutdown and collected November 2025 prices only in the second half of that month. October 2026 has no twelve-month change; November 2026's rests on a partial month.
  • The Northeast average price of white bread ends in April 2026 and is no longer shown.
  • EIA's 861M figures for 2025 and 2026 are preliminary. Con Edison's count of full-service residential customers in Form 861M rose by about 100,000 from November to December 2025, which may reflect customers returning from other suppliers.
  • The water bill for the year starting July 1, 2026, is the Department of Environmental Protection's proposed figure at the increase the Water Board adopted.

Updating

A routine runs on the 20th of each month, after BLS publishes the New York index. It pulls BLS, EIA, the Federal Housing Finance Agency and Zillow, rechecks the city-set prices against their sources and adds a change only when the agency's page confirms it. If a source fails, it stops and leaves the last good data in place, labeled with its month.
