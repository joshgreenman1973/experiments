What this is

Each line on the graphic takes one figure about New York City and finds a place or thing somewhere else whose comparable figure comes in at about the same number. The point is scale, so pairs are chosen for closeness first and familiarity second.

Two kinds of comparison, and why the mix matters

Most lines compare the same measure on both sides: subway riders against another city's subway riders, street trees against another city's street trees, dollars against dollars, hospital beds against hospital beds. These are the more informative pairings, because both numbers were produced to measure the same thing, and the counts row on the graphic says how many there are.

The rest compare a New York City count against a population, a length or a seating capacity, which carries no information about the measure and works only as a sense of magnitude. Those are kept where no peer figure came within reach: nobody else counts fire hydrants at New York City's scale, and the closest verified hydrant count anywhere was 37 percent off. Each of those lines is labelled in the counts row too, so the balance is visible rather than buried.

Where a same-measure comparison was found but landed outside about 11 percent, the population line was kept instead. Los Angeles County's homeless count, Pennsylvania's Medicaid rolls, Chicago's streetlights, Paris's cycle network and the West End's attendance were all checked and all missed, and the entries below say so.

How the pairs were made

Every number on both sides had to come from a page that was actually fetched during the research, and the exact words on that page containing the number are quoted below. Numbers that could not be fetched with a verbatim quote were left out, even when they were widely reported. Primary sources were preferred: the Census Bureau, city agencies and the Mayor's Management Report, transit operators, the city and state comptrollers, the International Monetary Fund's World Economic Outlook database for national GDP, the World Bank's population interface and national statistics offices. Where the only fetchable source was a news report quoting an agency, the entry says so.

The ratio on each line is the comparison figure divided by the New York City figure. The bar on the graphic draws New York City at full width and the comparison at that ratio. Gaps are shown to one decimal place under 2 percent and rounded otherwise.

What does not line up

Years differ on some lines because agencies publish on different schedules; each entry gives the year on both sides. National GDP figures for 2025 are IMF estimates in nominal dollars at market exchange rates, which move with currencies. Some city figures are rounded on the source page ("nearly 400,000 streetlights," "over 800 bridges and tunnels," "more than 30,000 acres") and the ratio is taken against the round number. Definitions are noted where they differ: authorized positions rather than employees, lane miles rather than centerline miles, homicides rather than murders, a fiscal-year average rather than a point-in-time count, a metropolitan area or a county rather than a city.

How to check it

Three Census Bureau links need a free API key to return data; the same tables are readable without one on data.census.gov and Census Reporter. The International Monetary Fund link serves one large JSON file of every country; search it for the three-letter country code. Follow the two links in any entry and search the page for the quoted words. The map pins each comparison at its capital, its city, or the thing itself; a straight-line distance is pinned at the far end of the line. Country outlines are Natural Earth 1:110m via the world-atlas package.
