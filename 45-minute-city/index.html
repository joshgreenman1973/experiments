This map was built with heavy use of an AI coding assistant. The travel times come from published Metropolitan Transportation Authority schedules and the street network comes from the city's own centerline file, but the code that stitches them together was largely machine-written and may contain errors. Treat it as a research sketch, not a trip planner. The methodology note explains exactly what is measured and what is assumed.

Methodology

This map answers one question: from a given spot in New York City, which streets can you actually reach inside a time budget? Every travel time below is computed from published schedules and the city's real street geometry. Nothing is hand-tuned to look right.

Ride times

For every pair of consecutive stops on every route, we take all scheduled trips in a time band and compute the median of departure-from-A to arrival-at-B. That is the ride time. It is a median of real timetable rows, not an estimate.

As a check, dividing straight-line distance by these times gives a median implied speed of 7.4 mph for buses and 15.1 mph for the subway. Those are lower bounds, since track and street distance always exceed straight-line distance. New York City buses average roughly 7 to 8 mph citywide, so the bus figure lands where it should. No segment implies a bus faster than 45 mph.

Waiting

This is the part most maps skip. For each stop, route and direction, we count scheduled departures in the time band and divide the band length by that count to get a headway. The wait is half the headway — the average for a rider who turns up without consulting a timetable.

Direction matters: a rider going uptown gains nothing from downtown trains. Counting both directions at one station would halve every subway wait, so departures are counted per direction.

You pay a wait when you board, and riding onward is free. A model that charged a wait at every stop would bill you thirty waits down Lexington Avenue.

The "longest wait you'd accept" slider caps this. At 20 minutes, an hourly bus contributes a 20-minute wait rather than 30, which is roughly how people behave with infrequent service: they consult the schedule instead of turning up blind.

Taxi

Cars are routed on a directed version of the same street network — one-way streets are honored, and highways, bridges, tunnels and ramps are included — at each segment's posted speed limit. Posted limits are a ceiling, not a description of traffic, so we slow them by a factor measured from real trips: a month of Taxi and Limousine Commission yellow-cab records (roughly 64,000 to 77,000 usable trips per time band, March 2026), each compared against the same zone-to-zone journey routed at posted speeds.

The factor is measured per borough from 11.9 million non-shared Uber and Lyft trips, because one citywide number flattered Manhattan and slandered the edges: at weekday midday Manhattan moves at 35% of posted limits (a 25 mph street is really an 8.8 mph street) while Queens moves at 47% and Staten Island at 68%. The cab also pays a measured pickup wait — about 4 minutes, the median request-to-pickup gap across every trip — because the train pays its platform wait and a fair race starts both clocks at the same moment.

Traffic is a control, not a constant: Light, Typical and Heavy are the 25th-percentile, median and 75th-percentile real trip for that borough and hour. From Washington Square at the morning rush a cab reaches 7,207 km of street in light traffic and 2,105 in heavy — a 3.4-fold swing — while the train's reach does not move at all, because a schedule does not care about traffic.

The "Train vs cab" view then answers the natural question directly: every street is colored by which door-to-door trip wins from your origin. The train's territory is the express spines; the cab's is the near field, the space between lines and everywhere the train cannot go in time. At 3am the train's territory nearly vanishes. In heavy morning traffic it inverts: the train wins 1,371 km against the cab's 626.

Two honest caveats. Yellow-cab trips skew heavily toward Manhattan, so the factor mostly describes Manhattan streets; outer-borough driving is somewhat faster than this map shows. And one factor per time band compresses the difference between a jammed crosstown street and a flowing expressway, so highway-heavy trips — to the airports especially — read slower than reality. Both errors run the same way: the taxi map is conservative. And any single ride varies enormously; the interquartile range of the calibration is reported in the data files.

The Staten Island Ferry

The ferry is included, and it carries more weight than any other single line here. It is run by the Department of Transportation rather than the Metropolitan Transportation Authority, so it lives in its own feed and is easy to miss — an earlier version of this map missed it, and the result was that Staten Island could not reach a single Manhattan subway station in an hour. The borough has 29 express bus routes over the Verrazzano, but on the schedules none of them does that trip in the time. Leaving the ferry out cut half a million people off from their own city.

The feed gives the real 25-minute crossing and real headways: every 18 minutes at rush hour, every 30 midday and overnight. From St George that works out to Whitehall in 34 minutes, South Ferry station at 39, Times Square at 60. Both terminals are accessible, so the ferry stays available with accessible transit switched on. Walking still cannot leave Staten Island, which is correct — there is no pedestrian path across the Verrazzano.

Accessibility

The "I require accessible transit" toggle limits subway boarding and exiting to the Metropolitan Transportation Authority's listed accessible stations — 171 of 496, per the MTA's own station file — while trains still ride through the rest. Every bus has a ramp. Walking routes on the street network minus step streets. Taxi/Uber becomes a wheelchair-accessible vehicle, with the wait measured rather than assumed: the Taxi and Limousine Commission logs a request and pickup timestamp for every Uber and Lyft trip, and the median request-to-pickup gap for accessible-vehicle requests in March 2026 runs 7 to 8 minutes by time of day, against about 4 for standard cars.

Access-A-Ride is deliberately absent: it books by 5pm the previous day, so it cannot appear in a map of where you can go from here, now.

Bridges

The city's centerline file types car-only expressway decks as bridges alongside walkable crossings. Every major crossing with real pedestrian access has dedicated path segments in the file (the Brooklyn Bridge promenade, the George Washington Bridge path), so same-named roadway decks are excluded from walking and biking; local drawbridges whose sidewalks live on the roadway stay. Staten Island, which genuinely has no pedestrian crossing, stands as its own walk network rather than being deleted for not touching the mainland's.

Walking and cycling

Both are routed on the actual street network — 199,859 nodes and 267,515 segments from the city centerline file. Walking uses streets, bridges, boardwalks, paths, step streets and alleys. Cycling uses the same minus step streets, which are stairs. Both exclude highways, ramps, driveways and ferry routes.

Default speeds are 3.1 mph walking and 8.9 mph cycling; both are sliders, because your speed is not our assumption to make.

An earlier version of this map assumed walking distance was always 1.4 times straight-line distance. We measured that: routing 2,395 random origin and destination pairs on the real network gives a median ratio of 1.31, with a range from 1.13 to 1.73. A single constant was never adequate, which is why this version routes the network instead of applying a factor.

What this does not model

  • Delays. These are schedules, not what ran. Real trips are worse.
  • Common lines. Each route's wait is computed on its own. In reality you board whichever train comes first, so where several routes serve the same trip — the 6 and the 6X, the 4, 5 and 6 down Lexington — the true wait is shorter than shown. This map is conservative there.
  • Crowding, elevators, stairs and fare gates. No penalty for entering a station or changing platforms.
  • Route branches. A rider who boards the A at a trunk stop can reach both Rockaway branches having paid only the trunk-level wait, so branch termini read slightly closer than reality — one of the few optimistic errors here.
  • Bike parking, bike lanes and hills. Cycling is routed on street geometry with a flat speed. It does not know that Broadway has a protected lane and that a bridge approach is a climb.
  • Weekend and overnight service changes. Planned diversions are not in the static feed.
  • Taxi pickup time, tolls and turn delays. The clock starts when the wheels do, and left turns cost the same as right ones.
  • Bikes plus transit. Bike mode is bike only. Transit modes walk to the stop.
  • NYC Ferry, PATH, Metro-North, Long Island Rail Road and New Jersey Transit. Not included. The Staten Island Ferry is.

Confidence

Ride times and headways: high confidence, straight from published schedules. Street routing: high confidence. Walking and cycling speeds: assumptions, exposed as sliders. Waiting model: standard practice, and deliberately conservative. The gap between this map and reality is mostly delay, and it runs in one direction — the real city is slower than this.
