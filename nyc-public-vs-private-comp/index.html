Who's really winning
the paycheck race?

Public-sector workers in New York City earn less per hour in wages than their private-sector counterparts — but when you add pensions, health insurance, and paid leave, the picture inverts. Explore the data, twenty-five years of it, and decide for yourself.

Updated · Sources: BLS QCEW, BLS ECEC, NYC Payroll

01Total compensation — NYC, 2024

Take-home pay is only one piece of what a worker actually earns. Once health insurance, pension contributions, paid leave, and payroll taxes are added in, the picture changes. NYC's private-sector advantage in total compensation is much smaller than the wage gap suggests — and at many agencies, public-sector workers come out ahead.

Mean vs median — and why it matters here. The mean adds every dollar and divides by headcount; a single $10M bonus pulls it up. 03The growth race — indexed to 2000

Raw dollar levels hide the pace of change. Rebased to 100 in the year 2000, which sector's wages have grown fastest? The result may surprise you: NYC's private sector, propelled by finance and tech compensation, has outpaced public wages nearly every year of the twenty-first century.

Hypothesis check. Over the full twenty-four years, NYC local-government wages grew slightly faster than private (+110% vs +100%), and state government grew dramatically faster. All three public sectors beat inflation (+82%). Private-sector wage gains were front-loaded in the 2000s — driven by the finance boom — and have underperformed public wage gains since roughly 2010. The benefits story (next panel) widens the public-sector advantage further.

04The benefits chasm

Wages are only part of compensation. The BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation survey measures, per hour, what employers spend on paid leave, insurance, retirement, and legally-required benefits (Social Security, Medicare, unemployment insurance). Public-sector benefits have always been richer — and the gap has widened dramatically over twenty years.

05The pension story

No single benefit has diverged more dramatically than retirement. In 2006, employers spent about $0.91 per hour funding private-sector retirement (mostly 401(k) matches). State and local government employers spent $2.54 — already nearly 3× more. By 2025, private retirement spending inched up to $1.53. Public retirement spending exploded to $8.54 per hour — much of it to cover unfunded pension obligations.

08Matched occupations — apples to apples

Aggregate means paper over everything that matters. A fairer comparison holds the occupation constant: an NYC government nurse versus a nurse at a private hospital; a city accountant versus a private-sector accountant. Three medians per occupation — NYC government workers, NYC-resident private-sector workers, and the broader metro area — tell three different stories.

NYC publicNYC privateNY-NJ-PA MSAMedian

Median annual base pay by occupation — three views

Teal: NYC government median regular gross pay, FY2025 annual-basis workers whose title matches the keyword (Citywide Payroll). Coral: NYC-resident private-sector median annual wages, full-time full-year workers, ACS 2019–2023 5-year PUMS (class-of-worker = private for-profit or non-profit; NYC PUMAs only). Light coral: BLS OEWS May 2024 median for the NY-NJ-PA metropolitan area, all employers. Delta compares NYC public to NYC private (PUMS) where the sample permits, otherwise to the MSA.
How to read this. The NYC private (PUMS) bar is the cleanest private comparator — it's actual NYC-resident private-sector workers, filtered to full-time full-year. The MSA bar includes all employers across the metro (NY + northern NJ + parts of PA); for nurses, accountants, lawyers, engineers, office staff it's a solid private-leaning benchmark, but for teachers, police, firefighters, and social workers it's dominated by public employment. When MSA and NYC-private diverge sharply (e.g. teachers: MSA $93k vs NYC private $65k), the MSA figure is reflecting public-school teachers region-wide while the PUMS figure captures private-school teachers in NYC.
Caveats. PUMS sample sizes shown as "n=" are unweighted microdata records; smaller n (under ~30) means the median is noisy. Some occupations (electricians n=17, plumbers n=11, civil engineers n=54) have thin samples even in the 5-year file. Title matching on the NYC public side is keyword-based, not SOC-coded — a title containing "NURSE" could include administrators. Hours worked differ by occupation (NYC teachers work a school-year calendar; private nurses often work 12-hour shifts). Neither source captures bonuses on the public side or benefits on either side. This is a base-salary snapshot, not total compensation.

09Methodology, data & caveats

Every number in this project is derived from a named, public data source. Nothing is modeled, imputed, or projected. Here's exactly where each figure comes from — and what it does not say.

Data sources

BLS QCEW
Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages, annual by-area ZIP files, 2000–2024. Aggregated across the five New York City counties (FIPS 36005, 36047, 36061, 36081, 36085). NAICS industry code 10 (total, all industries). Ownership codes: 1=Federal, 2=State, 3=Local, 5=Private. Employment-weighted means. "Local government" is broader than most people think: in QCEW it includes every local-government-owned employer with jobs in NYC — the NYC Department of Education, NYC Health + Hospitals (public benefit corp), the MTA (classified as local-gov ownership), CUNY community colleges, NYCHA, the public library systems, and smaller authorities. The 2024 QCEW local-gov headcount of ~486k is therefore much larger than the "mayoral-agency + DOE + uniformed" figure (~300k) commonly cited in city budget documents. Source: data.bls.gov/cew/data/files.
BLS ECEC
Employer Costs for Employee Compensation, quarterly time series via the BLS Public Data API. Series family CMU[ownership][item]000000000D. Private industry (ownership 2) and state & local government (ownership 3), 2006–2025. National coverage — not NYC-specific. For each calendar year we use Q1 data where available, else the earliest published quarter.
ACS PUMS
American Community Survey 2019–2023 5-year Public Use Microdata Sample, New York State person records. Filtered to NYC PUMAs (Bronx 3701–3710, Manhattan 3801–3810, Staten Island 3901–3903, Brooklyn 4001–4018, Queens 4101–4114). Additional filters: class of worker = private for-profit (COW=1) or private non-profit (COW=2); employed at work or with job (ESR=1,2,4,5); usual hours ≥35/week (WKHP); weeks worked ≥50 (WKWN); positive wages (WAGP). Occupation match uses 4-character SOC prefix on the SOCP field. Medians are person-weight-weighted (PWGTP). Source: www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/acs.
BLS OEWS
Occupational Employment & Wage Statistics, May 2024, New York-Newark-Jersey City NY-NJ-PA metropolitan statistical area (area 3562200). Published medians are across all employers (public + private). Used on the Matched occupations tab as the metro benchmark, with the caveat that public employment dominates in teacher/police/firefighter/social-worker occupations. Source: bls.gov/oes.
NYC Citywide Payroll
NYC Open Data dataset k397-673e, FY2014–FY2025. Fields used: fiscal_year, agency_name, regular_gross_paid, total_ot_paid, total_other_pay, pay_basis. Aggregates computed via Socrata SoQL directly on the server. Median is a Socrata median() function result. Source: data.cityofnewyork.us.

Definitions

Average annual wage (QCEW)
Total covered wages / annual average employment. Includes bonuses, tips, and stock-based compensation reported as wages. This is why NYC's private-sector mean looks high — finance bonuses flow through.
Regular gross paid (NYC)
Base pay actually disbursed in a fiscal year, before overtime and "other pay." Closer to take-home base salary than QCEW's total wages.
Total compensation (ECEC)
Wages + all employer-paid benefits per hour worked. Does NOT include employee-paid premiums, employee 401(k) contributions, or employer costs that aren't employment-based.
Benefits (ECEC)
Employer cost of paid leave + supplemental pay + insurance (life, health, disability) + retirement + legally required (Social Security, Medicare, unemployment, workers comp).

Limitations & what this tool does not claim

  • ECEC is national, not NYC-specific. Benefits data at the NYC geography is not published by BLS. Pension contributions, in particular, vary substantially by city (NYC's are among the highest due to heavy defined-benefit obligations).
  • QCEW private wages are inflated by bonus pay. A Manhattan hedge-fund analyst counts the same as a Queens retail worker in the employment count. Medians — which QCEW does not provide — would be a much lower figure.
  • QCEW covers jobs located in NYC, not NYC residents. A commuter from New Jersey working in Midtown is in the data; a Brooklyn resident commuting to Jersey City is not.
  • No occupation-matched comparisons. A proper public-vs-private comparison should hold occupation, education, and experience constant. That requires ACS PUMS microdata and is a planned future tab.
  • No inflation adjustment. All wage figures are nominal. Between 2000 and 2024, CPI-U rose roughly 85%.
  • NYC payroll data under-counts full-time earnings because it includes short-term and per-session workers. The "annual-basis only" filter on the histogram and agency tabs corrects for this.

Headline takeaways

  1. Nominal NYC private-sector wages have grown faster than public over 25 years — contra the common narrative that public pay has surged.
  2. Public-sector benefits, especially retirement, have grown dramatically faster than private — mostly driven by pension funding requirements.
  3. At the total-compensation level, state & local government workers nationally out-earn private workers ($65.68/hr vs $46.15/hr as of Dec 2025).
  4. Inside the NYC government, mean and median pay vary enormously by agency — headline averages conceal large distributions.
