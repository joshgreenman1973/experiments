The tide line

Under the high-end sea-level projection used in the city's new maps, the average daily high tide would cover land holding about 63,000 homes by the 2100s, up from about 1,200 in the 2050s.

The city published new maps of future flooding on Nov. 13. They show, for five future decades, the land the average daily high tide would cover and the land a 100-year or 500-year coastal storm would flood. The 100-year storm floodplain covers 55 square miles and lots holding 278,000 homes in the 2030s map, and 82 square miles and 537,000 homes in the 2100s map. The 2150s map assumes 177 inches of sea-level rise. In it, the daily high tide alone covers 85 square miles, with about 602,000 homes, about one in six in the city.

How this was made

The maps. The Mayor's Office of Climate and Environmental Justice built the layers and posted them to NYC Open Data as Future High Tides and Future Floodplains, made public Nov. 13, 2026. Both use the 90th-percentile sea-level-rise projections from the fourth New York City Panel on Climate Change (2024): 13 inches for the 2030s, 23 for the 2050s, 45 for the 2080s, 65 for the 2100s and 177 for the 2150s. The panel puts roughly a 1-in-10 chance on the sea rising more than that, so these are high-end scenarios.

What the lines mean. The high-tide line is mean higher high water, the average height of the higher of each day's two high tides, raised by the projected rise. Land below it would be under water on a typical day's higher tide. The storm floodplains start from FEMA's 2015 preliminary flood maps and add the same rise. All of it is a "bathtub" model on a 10-foot elevation grid: water spreads to any land connected to the shoreline that sits below the water level. The city's own documents say the model ignores sea walls and other future defenses, erosion, sinking land, groundwater, rain and drainage, and that piers and wharves missing from the elevation grid can look flooded. The city says the layers are for information and not precise enough for site-level planning.

Homes. Counts are residential units from the city's PLUTO tax-lot file (version 26v2), counted for every lot whose center point falls inside a line. A large lot whose center sits on a flooded edge counts in full, and a lot that floods only at its edge does not count at all. The address lookup uses the same lot test, plus a check of the exact address point against the map.

Map. Polygons were simplified to 6 feet (the source grid is 10 feet) for the web map. Lot counts use the full-detail polygons.
