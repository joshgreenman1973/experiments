The tide line

Under the high-end sea-level projection used in the city's new maps, the average daily high tide would cover land holding about 63,000 homes by 2100, up from about 1,200 in the 2050s. Half of that 2050s figure is two large waterfront lots.

The city published new maps of future flooding on Nov. 13. They show, for five future decades, the land the average daily high tide would cover and the land a 100-year or 500-year coastal storm would flood. The 100-year storm floodplain covers 55 square miles and lots holding 278,000 homes in the 2030s map, and 82 square miles and 537,000 homes in the 2100 map. The 2150 map assumes 177 inches of sea-level rise. In it, the daily high tide alone covers 85 square miles, with about 602,000 homes, about one in six in the city. Those maps use only the high-end projection from the city's climate panel; under its lower projections, the same water levels arrive decades later, or not by 2150. The "Pick a projection" tab below shows them on NOAA's sea-level maps.

How this was made

The maps. The Mayor's Office of Climate and Environmental Justice built the layers and posted them to NYC Open Data as Future High Tides and Future Floodplains, made public Nov. 13, 2026. Both use the 90th-percentile sea-level-rise projections from the fourth New York City Panel on Climate Change (2024): 13 inches for the 2030s, 23 for the 2050s, 45 for the 2080s, 65 for 2100 and 177 for 2150. The 90th percentile is the panel's high estimate across the climate scenarios it pooled; it does not assign odds to it. (The city's files label the last two the 2100s and 2150s; the panel's figures are for those years.)

What the lines mean. The high-tide line is mean higher high water, the average height of the higher of each day's two high tides, raised by the projected rise. Land below it would be under water on a typical day's higher tide. The storm floodplains start from FEMA's 2015 preliminary flood maps and add the same rise. All of it is a "bathtub" model on a 10-foot elevation grid: water spreads to any land connected to the shoreline that sits below the water level. The city's documents say the shoreline includes existing coastal structures, and that the model leaves out erosion, sinking land, groundwater, rainfall, and future construction or changes in land use. Piers and wharves missing from the elevation grid can look flooded. The 100-year layer includes wave effects and the 500-year layer does not, so a few lots fall inside the 100-year line but outside the 500-year one. The city says the layers are for information and not precise enough for site-level planning.

Homes. Counts are residential units from the city's PLUTO tax-lot file (version 26v2), counted for every lot whose center point falls inside a line. A large lot whose center sits on a flooded edge counts in full, and a lot that floods only at its edge does not count at all. Two waterfront lots, 184 Kent Avenue in Williamsburg (333 units) and 70 New Lane on Staten Island (277), count from the 2030s map because their center points sit within 55 feet of the shore; they are 72% of the 2030s high-tide count and half of the 2050s count. The address lookup reports the earlier of two tests, the lot's center point and the exact address point, in both tabs.

Other projections. The city mapped only the panel's 90th-percentile projection. The "Pick a projection" tab uses NOAA's sea-level rise maps instead, which show the daily high tide at each foot of rise from 0 to 10 feet, and the panel's own table of projections at the 10th, 25th, 75th and 90th percentiles (NYC Open Data; the table has no median). NOAA measures from the average high tide of 1983–2001 and the panel from 1995–2014, so the page adds 2.3 inches, the observed rise in average higher high water at the Battery between those periods, before matching a projection to the nearest whole foot. Rounding to whole feet can move a projection by up to about 6 inches, which can change the home count a lot; picking a cell shows the counts on both sides. NOAA's maps and the city's also draw the shoreline and the elevation differently, so at the same rise the two lines and counts differ. NOAA has no storm floodplains, so that tab shows the daily high tide only.

Map. Polygons were simplified to 6 feet (the source grid is 10 feet) for the web map. Lot counts use the full-detail polygons.
