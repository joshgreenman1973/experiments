More women than ever own firearms. More women than ever are killed by them. The full picture is more complicated than either side admits.

Women are the fastest-growing group of gun owners in America

Once a tiny fraction of gun owners, women are now the fastest-growing demographic in the American firearms market. The COVID-19 pandemic and social upheaval of 2020 accelerated a trend that had been building for a decade.

Black women have been among the fastest-growing new buyers

In 2021, nearly 87% of firearm retailers reported increased sales to Black women — the largest increase reported for any demographic group. Of all Americans who became first-time owners from 2019 to 2021, about 1 in 5 (20–21%) were Black, a share far exceeding the Black share of the general population. The reasons cited most often: rising violent crime, social unrest and a distrust that police would protect them.

Source: NSSF, 2021; Annals of Internal Medicine, 2021

About 72% of gun owners overall cite protection as a major reason they own a firearm — and among women owners specifically the share is similar, about 71%. Across surveys, women consistently rank self-defense higher than men do. Pew Research Center, 2023 (overall); Pew, 2017 (women)

An abuser's access to a gun is the single strongest predictor of whether he will kill her

Intimate-partner gun homicides of women are rising, not falling

Two-thirds of all intimate-partner homicides are committed with a firearm, and 77% of intimate-partner firearm-homicide victims are women. Among women killed with a gun, 61% were killed by an intimate partner.

The trend is worsening. From 2014 to 2023, intimate-partner homicides of women rose about 22% — with firearm cases rising 36% even as homicides by all other means rose just 3%.

The racial disparity is stark: Black women are about 3.5 times as likely to be fatally shot by a partner as white women. For Black women aged 18–34, the rate is nearly six times higher.

Source: Everytown (77%, 22% rise, racial disparities); Violence Policy Center (61%); CDC MMWR (two-thirds firearm)

Many women who straw-purchase guns are themselves victims

Reporting from the Star Tribune and The Trace has documented how women in abusive relationships are coerced into buying firearms for partners who are legally prohibited from owning them. They have clean records. Their abusers do not.

In Philadelphia, women accounted for 24% of 559 straw-purchasing defendants since 2018 — despite committing less than 10% of the city's shootings. Prevention programs like Operation LIPSTICK now target outreach specifically to women about the dangers and consequences of straw purchasing.

Source: The Trace; The Philadelphia Citizen

Women buy guns for protection — but the research says gun ownership makes women less safe, not more

The number-one reason women buy guns is self-defense. But peer-reviewed studies consistently find that living with a handgun owner dramatically increases a woman's risk of being shot.

The promise
71% of women gun owners cite self-defense as a major reason for ownership
Women's concealed-carry permits growing at roughly 2x the rate of men's
~60,000–65,000 defensive gun uses per year (NCVS estimate, all genders)
The research
People living with handgun owners are ~7x more likely to be shot by a spouse or intimate partner — and 84% of those victims are women
Living with a handgun owner roughly doubles the risk of dying by homicide
About 1% of violent-crime victims use a gun in self-defense
Source: Pew, 2017; Crime Prevention Research Center, 2024; Stanford Medicine, 2022; AJPH / NCVS analysis
For every justifiable homicide by a civilian using a firearm, there are roughly 34 criminal gun homicides, dozens of gun suicides and several accidental gun deaths. Violence Policy Center, using FBI and CDC data

A patchwork of laws tries to keep guns from domestic abusers — but most states don't require surrender

Federal law and a landmark Supreme Court ruling have strengthened protections, but enforcement gaps leave millions of women at risk.

1996
The Lautenberg Amendment prohibits gun possession by anyone convicted of misdemeanor domestic violence — covering categories such as spouses, co-parents and cohabitants, but not dating partners who never married, lived with, or had a child with the victim.
2003
The landmark Campbell et al. study establishes that an abuser's gun access is associated with roughly fivefold higher odds of intimate-partner homicide.
2022
The Bipartisan Safer Communities Act partially closes the “boyfriend loophole” by extending the misdemeanor-DV gun ban to dating partners — but for that newly covered category only, a first-time offender's rights can be restored after five years with no further offenses.
2024
In United States v. Rahimi, the Supreme Court upholds (8–1) the federal ban on gun possession under domestic-violence restraining orders.

Gun culture has been remade in a female image — from “mama bear” marketing to concealed-carry fashion

The cultural landscape of women and guns has transformed in a single generation: women's shooting leagues, a concealed-carry fashion industry and two opposing maternal movements battling over the future of gun policy.

Gun ads targeting women evolved from objectification to empowerment in a decade
Pre-2008
Ads targeted almost exclusively at men. Women rarely shown; when present, in passive or sexualized roles.
2008–2012
Women appear in ads but still mainly sexualized — aimed at men buying guns “for her.”
2013–2015
A major shift: women depicted as competent, empowered users. “The Domestic Defender” and “The Capable Carrier” frames emerge.
2016–2020
The “Serious Student” frame dominates about 45% of the ads studied. Women shown training, learning and mastering firearms.
