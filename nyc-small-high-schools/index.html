Most small schools today are established schools that shrank

The Bloomberg administration closed dozens of large high schools and opened scores of small ones in their buildings (Chalkbeat). The new schools typically opened with a ninth-grade class and added a grade a year, so for their first few years they were small by design; the state enrollment files show them as schools with no seniors yet. In 2004-05, — of the — schools under 250 were still phasing in. In 2025-26, — of — were established schools with a senior class.

What small schools cost

Average spending per student generally falls as schools get bigger. Part of the gap is the students: schools under 250 enroll larger shares of students with disabilities and English learners than schools with 500 to 999 students, and those students carry extra weight in the city's Fair Student Funding formula (slide 10, which lists the weights proposed for 2026-27). Small schools also have minimum staffing and operating costs spread across fewer students. Those costs may help explain the rest of the gap, alongside the city's funding protections and differences the model does not capture. The Education Department's FY2027 Fair Student Funding presentation lists a flat — foundation per school on top of per-student money (slide 9).

How this was done

Which schools count

School enrollment comes from the New York State Education Department's public school enrollment files, 1994-95 through 2025-26 (BEDS day, the state's Basic Educational Data System count date; all students). Each year's count is taken in the fall: the 2025-26 counts are as of Oct. 1, 2025, in a file the state pulled on Oct. 8, 2026. A high school here is a New York City public school, not a charter, where grades 9 to 12 make up at least a quarter of enrollment. That keeps 6-12 schools and drops elementary and middle schools with a stray ninth grader. Excluded: District 75 special education schools, District 79 alternative programs, the pre-2004 alternative high school district (state code 77), schools through 2009-10 that share a borough and school number with one of that district's schools, and every school the Education Department has labeled a transfer high school in its progress reports (2006-07 to 2012-13) or School Quality Reports (2014-15 to 2023-24). The transfer list pools those designations and applies them to every year, including 2025-26; it was not checked against each year's designations. Size is total enrollment, including middle school grades at 6-12 schools, as in Chalkbeat's chart. School years are labeled by span; Chalkbeat appears to label them by the fall year.

New vs. established

A school counts as still phasing in if it has no 12th graders and first appears in the state files less than four years earlier. That is a proxy from enrollment, not a register of opening dates, and "established" is everything else. Schools are followed across the 2003-04 recode by borough and school number, and transfer labels from later years are applied to earlier ones, so a few schools' histories may be misassigned.

Spending

Spending per student is the state's 2024-25 school-level figure (federal, state and local; report card database, table "Expenditures per Pupil"). The breakdown comes from each school's 2024-25 Financial Transparency Report on data.nysed.gov (example: Murry Bergtraum): instruction is functions 1 and 3, pupil support function 2, school administration function 5, shared costs functions 4 and 6 to 12. Needs shares come from the state's 2024-25 enrollment files for students with disabilities, English language learners and economically disadvantaged students. The state suppresses some small subgroup counts, and a suppressed count is not a zero. For the 23 schools in the spending analysis whose English learner count is suppressed, the model uses total enrollment minus the published count of students who are not English learners (41 students across the 23 schools, zero to four at each). Those derived counts are used only inside the model; the school-level files leave them blank, as the state does. The $225,000 foundation and the funding weights are from the Education Department's FY2027 Fair Student Funding presentation (slides 9 and 10); slide 10 labels the weights as proposed for 2026-27, so the presentation alone does not establish the final adopted weights.

The size premium

A regression of log spending per student on size group plus the three needs shares and a flag for schools with middle grades (420 schools; reference group 500-999 students; robust standard errors). It shows an association between size and spending among schools that look alike on those measures, not what small size itself causes. Each school's premium is its predicted spending at its own size minus its predicted spending at 500-999, times its enrollment. The dollar figures convert predicted log spending back to dollars, which estimates typical rather than average spending; a standard adjustment for that would raise them by about 0.3 percent. A smooth version using log enrollment instead of size groups gives a smaller estimate for schools under 250 (—). The two differ partly because the grouped model lets the extra cost climb steeply at the smallest sizes and partly because they use different benchmarks: one school of exactly 500 students, against the 500-999 group as a whole. The 2023-24 data show a somewhat steeper size gradient than 2024-25, but that check reuses 2024-25 needs shares, so it is not fully independent. The city's hold harmless policy, in place since 2023-24, has protected most of a school's prior-year funding at the initial allocation even as enrollment falls (city comptroller, FY2027 budget comments). Some of the small-school premium may reflect that policy; the model cannot say how much.

What this cannot show

The functions grouped here as shared costs are nearly flat per student across size groups. These data cannot show whether that reflects how the state's reports allocate those costs or what buildings and central services actually cost small schools. The state's figures are operating spending and leave out debt service and some other items. The analysis cannot say what a merger would save, which depends on which staff and buildings are kept. Class sizes, adult attention and course offerings, the breadth problem in the Chalkbeat story, are not in these data.

What changed on Nov. 6, 2026

After an outside fact-check, this page was revised. The lead now says 2025-26, the school year of the latest count; it had said "this fall," but the count date was Oct. 1, 2025. Language saying the remaining spending gap is the fixed cost of running a school, and that the extra money buys smaller classes but not more courses, was removed because the analysis does not measure those things. The comparison with Chalkbeat no longer offers an explanation for the difference, and Chalkbeat's count is now given from its chart data as 113, not about 112. Suppressed English learner counts, which had been treated as zero, are now handled as described above; that moved the under-250 dollar estimate from $55.6 million to $55.5 million (shown rounded as $56 million before and $55 million now) and left the 18 percent premium unchanged after rounding. The smooth model's estimate is now shown as $23 million; the page had rounded it to about $24 million. The 95 percent confidence range and further notes on method were added.
