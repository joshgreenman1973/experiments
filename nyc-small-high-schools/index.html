Small used to mean new. Now it means shrinking.

The Bloomberg administration closed dozens of large high schools and opened scores of small ones in their buildings (Chalkbeat). The new schools typically opened with a ninth-grade class and added a grade a year, so for their first few years they were small by design; the state enrollment files show them as schools with no seniors yet. In 2004-05, — of the — schools under 250 were still phasing in. In 2025-26, — of — were established schools with a senior class.

What small schools cost

Spending per student falls steadily as schools get bigger. Part of that gap is the students: the smallest schools enroll more students with disabilities and English learners, who get extra money through the city's Fair Student Funding weights (slide 10). The rest is the fixed cost of running a school. A principal, an office, a guidance counselor and a full slate of required courses cost about the same whether 150 or 1,500 students show up. The city builds some of this into its funding formula: in 2026-27, every school gets a flat — on top of its per-student money (NYC Public Schools Fair Student Funding presentation, slide 9).

Why these counts are lower than Chalkbeat's

Chalkbeat's chart shows about 112 high schools under 250 students in 2025. Its note says transfer schools are excluded, but its totals match the state data closely only when transfer schools are counted. When New York reorganized its school codes in 2003-04, the old alternative high school district, where transfer schools had been grouped, went away, so a filter on that district stops working after 2003. There are — schools in this year's state files that the Education Department classifies as transfer high schools in its School Quality Reports and earlier progress reports, most of them small. Leaving them in gives — schools under 250 this year; taking them out gives —. It is worth asking Chalkbeat's data team before citing either figure.

How this was done

Which schools count

School enrollment comes from the New York State Education Department's public school enrollment files, 1994-95 through 2025-26 (BEDS day, all students). A high school here is a New York City public school, not a charter, where grades 9 to 12 make up at least a quarter of enrollment. That keeps 6-12 schools and drops elementary and middle schools with a stray ninth grader. Excluded: District 75 special education schools, District 79 alternative programs, the pre-2004 alternative high school district (state code 77) and its successor schools, and every school the Education Department has labeled a transfer high school in its progress reports (2006-07 to 2012-13) or School Quality Reports (2014-15 to 2023-24). Size is total enrollment, including middle school grades at 6-12 schools, as in Chalkbeat's chart. School years are labeled by span; Chalkbeat appears to label them by the fall year.

New vs. established

A school counts as still phasing in if it has no 12th graders and first appears in the state files less than four years earlier. Schools are followed across the 2003-04 recode by borough and school number.

Spending

Spending per student is the state's 2024-25 school-level figure (federal, state and local; report card database, table "Expenditures per Pupil"). The breakdown comes from each school's 2024-25 Financial Transparency Report on data.nysed.gov (example: Murry Bergtraum): instruction is functions 1 and 3, pupil support function 2, school administration function 5, shared costs functions 4 and 6 to 12. Needs shares come from the state's 2024-25 enrollment files for students with disabilities, English language learners and economically disadvantaged students. The $225,000 foundation and the funding weights are from the Education Department's FY2027 Fair Student Funding presentation (slides 9 and 10).

The size premium

A regression of log spending per student on size group plus the three needs shares and a flag for schools with middle grades (420 schools; reference group 500-999 students). Each school's premium is its predicted spending at its own size minus its predicted spending at 500-999, times its enrollment. A smooth version using log enrollment instead of size groups gives a smaller estimate for schools under 250 (about $24 million), because the extra cost climbs steeply only at the smallest sizes. The 2023-24 data show a steeper size gradient than 2024-25. These figures include the effect of the city's hold harmless policy, which since 2023-24 has guaranteed that schools start each year with no less money than the year before even as enrollment falls (city comptroller, FY2027 budget comments), so part of today's small-school premium is that policy.

What this cannot show

The state's report spreads many costs evenly per student, so it understates how much buildings and central services cost small schools. It cannot say what a merger would save, which depends on which staff and buildings are kept. Course offerings, the breadth problem in the Chalkbeat story, are not in these data.
