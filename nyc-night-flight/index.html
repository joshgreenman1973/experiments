How this works

Birds overhead

The nightly counts come from the BirdCast Migration Dashboard, run by the Cornell Lab of Ornithology, Colorado State University and the University of Massachusetts Amherst. BirdCast reads the National Weather Service's weather radars, separates birds from rain and insects and estimates how many birds crossed each county in the night. Each borough is a county, so each gets its own estimate. The figures are estimates from radar, not head counts, and BirdCast does not identify species.

BirdCast's dashboard does not offer a download. This page reads the numbers each dashboard page carries for its own charts, one county and one night at a time (dashboard.birdcast.org/region/<county>?night=<date>). A night's total is the change in BirdCast's running season total from one night to the next; it matches the "total birds" figure BirdCast shows for that night. The historical average is the same calculation on BirdCast's average series. BirdCast serves one average series whatever year is asked for, so past seasons are compared with the average as BirdCast publishes it today. The 10-minute detail (birds in flight, direction, speed and altitude) is collected from spring 2026 on.

The five borough estimates are not added together. A bird that crosses Brooklyn and then Manhattan is counted in both, so a sum would count many birds twice. For the city as a whole, the page uses the largest borough's estimate each night and calls it "at least": every bird that crossed that borough crossed the city.

BirdCast's live feed runs March 1 to June 15 and Aug. 1 to Nov. 15. Its county dashboard goes back to fall 2021.

Birds on the ground: eBird

The next-day species counts in the chart, the first-of-the-season arrivals and the species list for each night come from eBird, the Cornell Lab of Ornithology's database of birders' checklists, through the eBird API. For each borough and day the page reads eBird's list of species reported that day. Species don't add up across boroughs, so the citywide list is every species reported in any borough. Each night is paired with reports dated the next day. "First of the season" means a species' first eBird report in that season in the city (or in the selected borough) and is not shown in a season's first two weeks. eBird reports cover spring 2024 on. eBird allows 500 requests an hour, so seasons are filled in a few days at a time by the daily update; until a season is complete, and for earlier seasons, the chart shows iNaturalist counts instead. Reports reflect how many birders went out, which rises on weekends and in good weather and peaks in May. The species numbers measure what birders found, not how many birds there were. eBird's terms restrict passing its data along in its original form, so the page stores only this daily summary.

Birds on the ground: iNaturalist

iNaturalist sightings are observations of birds inside iNaturalist's New York City boundary (place 674), "verifiable" grade only: research grade plus needs-ID, which leaves out captive birds and records without a photo, sound, date or place. Subspecies are folded into their species; sightings not identified to species count toward the sightings total but not the species count. Each night is paired with sightings dated the next day. "First of the season" means a species' first iNaturalist sighting in the city that season, and is not shown for a season's first two weeks, when nearly every species is new to the list. iNaturalist depends on who goes out with a camera: weekends and good weather bring more sightings, and the most recent weeks fill in as people upload and identify. Recent days are refreshed for 30 days.

Species photos, including for eBird's species list (matched by scientific name), are iNaturalist's default photo for each species, shown only when the photographer licensed it under Creative Commons or CC0, with credit under "Photo credits." They are not necessarily photos taken in New York City.

Wind

Wind is hourly 10-meter wind at Central Park (40.78, -73.97) from Open-Meteo's historical weather API, averaged over 7 p.m. to 6 a.m. as a vector, so a night that swings from northwest to northeast averages to north. A night is marked orange when the average wind has a clear component blowing the way migrants travel: from the north in fall and from the south in spring (the cosine of the direction the wind comes from is above 0.3 in fall or below -0.3 in spring). Ground-level wind at one spot is a rough stand-in for the winds aloft that birds actually ride. The comparison above is a plain average of nights, not a model, and it does not account for other weather or the calendar.

Updates

A scheduled job checks every morning. During BirdCast's seasons it adds the night just ended for each borough, refreshes the season's totals, the last 30 days of eBird and iNaturalist reports and the wind. It fails, rather than publishing stale numbers as new, when BirdCast has no data for three days running in season.
