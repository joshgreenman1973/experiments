What Does Dinner
Actually Cost?

What does dinner out cost a family of four in New York City? Each month we add up a real bill — two adult entrees, two kid portions, two non-alcoholic drinks, plus tax and tip — at a panel of everyday family restaurants across the five boroughs, re-pricing the same dishes so the change is genuinely apples-to-apples.

The bill we add up — and how we source it

A note on what this can and can't see. We can only track restaurants that post a readable menu with prices online — which is roughly one in five family restaurants we check. That tilts the panel toward more-digitized spots and away from the cash-only, menu-on-the-wall immigrant places that are often the most affordable. So read this as a trend for the restaurants we can follow, not a census of the cheapest dinner in the city. We use each restaurant's own menu or direct ordering page — never delivery-app prices, which run 15–25% higher and move with platform fees, not the kitchen.

Sources & Methodology

Where the prices come from

Every price on this page comes from the restaurant's own menu — either the printed menu posted in-store or the menu on the restaurant's official website. We do not use third-party delivery platforms (Grubhub, DoorDash, Uber Eats, Seamless), where menu prices are routinely marked up 15–40% above dine-in pricing.

How restaurants were selected

  • 250+ restaurants across all five NYC boroughs, weighted toward neighborhood spots families actually use — not tasting-menu destinations or tourist traps.
  • Every restaurant is validated against the Google Places API (New): must exist, must be currently open, must have at least 25 Google reviews, and the stored location must match Google's coordinates within 500 meters.
  • A restaurant is treated as closed only on a real signal — Google marks it permanently closed, or its page returns 404/410. A menu that simply can't be read (JavaScript-rendered, bot-blocked) is a scrape failure, never a closure.

How the bill is built

For each restaurant we pin two specific dishes — an adult entree and a kid portion — chosen by an AI pass and then adversarially double-checked by a second AI (which rejects, for example, a whole specialty pizza miscast as a kid meal, or a price that can't be a real portion). The monthly bill is:

(2 × adult entree + 2 × kid portion + 2 × beverage) × (1 + tax + tip)

  • Apples-to-apples by construction. Each month we re-fetch the menu and re-price those same two dishes by deterministic string match — no AI at re-check time. We only ever compare a dish to its own past price (the matched-model method the CPI uses).
  • Median headline. The big number is the median bill across the panel, which a few pricey institutions (a $29 pastrami sandwich) can't drag around; we show the average and the 25th–75th percentile range alongside.
  • Tax & tip, standardized drinks. 8.875% NYC sales tax and an 18% tip are included. Beverages are fixed at $2.50 each citywide (menus rarely list soda prices), so drinks add no false movement.

How we keep it honest

  • Direct prices only — never delivery. We read each restaurant's own website or direct ordering page. We never use Grubhub/DoorDash/Uber Eats (or Grubhub-owned MenuPages/Allmenus): their menu prices run 15–25% higher and move with platform commissions, not the kitchen.
  • Same-item only. If a pinned dish is gone or unreadable in a given month, we carry its last price forward and flag it rather than substituting a different dish.
  • Mis-read guard. A dish that appears to jump beyond ±100% is almost always a scrape error; we cap and flag it rather than let it whipsaw the bill.
  • Freshness signal. We record each page's last-modified date, so a price that "holds" on a website nobody has touched in months is flagged — website staleness isn't mistaken for a stable price, the central risk for any web-menu tracker.
  • Price ceiling. The panel targets entrees under $25; the handful of signature dishes above it (Katz's pastrami, a chicken parm) are marked with a ● so you can see them.

Selection bias — what this can and can't see

We can only track restaurants that post a readable menu with prices online, which is roughly one in five of the family restaurants we check. That tilts the panel toward more-digitized spots and away from the cash-only, menu-on-the-wall immigrant places that are often the most affordable food in the city. Read this as a trustworthy trend for the restaurants we can follow — not a census of the cheapest dinner in New York. Closing that gap would take occasional on-the-ground price checks, which this automated panel doesn't do.

Benchmark

For context we show the official yardstick: the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics' "food away from home" CPI for the New York–Newark–Jersey City metro, which rose 3.4% over the 12 months ending April 2026. Our panel is more local and granular; how it diverges from the BLS series is itself the story.

Cadence and revisions

The bill is re-checked on a fixed monthly cadence, run manually — there is no always-on automated fetcher. Published months are not silently revised: once a month is posted it stays, and any correction is noted. Month-to-month moves are noisy at this panel size; year-over-year will be the more meaningful comparison once twelve months exist.

Known limitations

  • The panel measures the bill over time for a fixed set of trackable restaurants — not the citywide price level, and not a representative sample (see selection bias above).
  • Borough coverage is uneven because readable menus are unevenly distributed.
  • Digital-menu restaurants reprice more cheaply than printed-menu spots, so the panel may move a bit sooner and more often than the city as a whole.
  • A renamed or removed dish drops out until it is re-pinned by hand; drinks are standardized, not measured.
