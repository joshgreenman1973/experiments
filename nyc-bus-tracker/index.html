Methodology and comparison to MTA official metrics

Positions come from the MTA BusTime API. Speeds here are our own estimates, not official MTA figures.

Speed

Live map: each time the page polls the feed, every bus seen in the previous poll gets a speed: distance traveled divided by elapsed time. Distance is measured along the route's shape where the shape has loaded, and straight-line otherwise. Readings of 60 mph or more are discarded as GPS errors. Each route's speed is the average of its buses, and the system figure is the plain average of routes, so every route counts equally.

Weekly figures: a scheduled job records the whole fleet in short bursts twice an hour, from about 6 a.m. to midnight. For each bus seen in two snapshots no more than 10 minutes apart, it divides straight-line distance by elapsed time, dropping readings under 0.5 mph (parked or laying over) and over 60 mph. Each hour of the day gets equal weight, so weeks compare fairly even when collection missed some hours. A week counts only if all seven days are present and at least half the hours were captured.

Checked against the MTA: for every month both cover, the tracker's weekly method has been compared with the MTA's published hourly route speeds (data.ny.gov r6db-kkzj). Ranked from slowest to fastest, the routes come out in nearly the same order (rank correlation 0.99). On local routes the tracker reads about 9% faster than the MTA, most likely because it drops time buses spend stopped, which the MTA counts. Staten Island express routes read slower, for reasons not yet known. The full comparison, refreshed weekly, is on Round and Round.

What this page no longer shows

Earlier versions showed bunching, big gaps and an estimated rider wait. An audit in September 2026 found those numbers could not be defended. The bunching count flagged a Limited bus passing a local on the same route, and buses parked together at the end of the line. The wait and gap estimates lined buses up on a north-south or east-west axis rather than along the route (wrong in 45% of cases tested), measured straight lines between them, and skipped routes with fewer than three buses running. They have been removed while a replacement wait measure is built.

General caveats

  • Positions are GPS readings relayed by the MTA; the feed occasionally drops a bus or reports a stale position.
  • The tracker has no schedule data, so it cannot say whether buses are on time.
  • Routes are grouped into boroughs by the letters in their names. Express routes (BM, BxM, QM, SIM, X) are kept out of the borough averages.

This tracker was built with AI assistance (Claude). Speed figures are our own estimates derived from GPS positions, not official MTA metrics; ridership comes from the MTA's passenger-counter data. Automated pipelines can contain errors — check the methodology and the source data before republishing any number.

Geography cuts
Two ways of slicing the same weekly speed data by where routes run. Bus priority corridors: routes whose shape spends at least 30% of its length within about 60 meters of a marked bus lane or busway (NYC DOT bus lane data), against routes with little or none (≤10%). Route and lane geometry are both densified to 15-meter spacing first, so this is a share of length rather than of raw map vertices. Routes between 10% and 30% appear in neither tile. Proximity is not proof a route uses a lane — a lane on a cross street it merely crosses can register. Congestion zone: routes the MTA lists as operating in or crossing the Central Business District, against everything else. Each figure is the simple mean of member routes' weekly average speeds — directional, not hour-normalized. Compare each group with its own past, not group against group: bus lanes are installed where congestion is worst and congestion-zone routes run the densest streets, so a raw cross-group gap says more about geography than about the lanes.

Data collection — how it runs and what to be skeptical of

Loading collection stats…

How collection runs. A GitHub Actions job starts at 9 and 39 minutes past each hour, from about 6 a.m. to midnight Eastern. Each run polls the MTA BusTime API about a dozen times over six minutes, recording every bus in service. GitHub sometimes delays or skips scheduled runs, so which hours get captured varies; the coverage column in the table shows how much of each week was caught. Nothing is interpolated.

How speed is computed (collector/process.js, nightly). For each bus seen in two snapshots no more than 10 minutes apart: straight-line distance divided by elapsed time, keeping readings from 0.5 to 60 mph. Each route's speed is the mean of its buses' readings, and the system figure is the plain mean of routes. Weekly and monthly figures (collector/rollup.js) give each hour of the day equal weight, so a week that caught more fast early-morning hours doesn't look faster than it was.

Known limits.

  • On local routes the weekly speed reads about 9% faster than the MTA's published figure, most likely because readings under 0.5 mph (buses stopped at stops and lights) are dropped. Routes rank in nearly the same order as the MTA's. See Round and Round for the monthly comparison.
  • Borough averages leave out express routes (BM, BxM, QM, SIM, X). Routes are assigned to a borough by the letters in their names.
  • Weeks with fewer than seven days or under 50% of hours captured are marked with an asterisk and should not be compared with full weeks.
  • The data files still carry bunching, gap and wait fields from the original pipeline. An audit found them unreliable, and they are no longer shown or exported to CSV.

Where the data lives. Raw snapshots: the bus-data branch of the repo, one file per hour. Daily roll-ups: data/daily/. Weekly and monthly: data/summary/. The math is in collector/process.js and collector/rollup.js.
