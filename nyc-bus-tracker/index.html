Methodology and comparison to MTA official metrics

Positions come from the Metropolitan Transportation Authority’s BusTime feed. Speeds here are our own estimates, not official MTA figures.

Speed with stops

Every speed on this site is distance over time with time stopped at stops and lights included, because that is what a rider experiences. A bus seen in two snapshots a half-minute or so apart gives one reading: the straight-line distance between its two GPS positions over the time between its two GPS timestamps. Readings from a bus on layover at the end of the line, readings where the bus’s position did not refresh, and readings of 60 mph or more are dropped. For each route and hour, speed is total distance over total time. Until October 2026 the site showed moving speed only, leaving out time stopped; that figure ran about 9% faster than the MTA’s and is still published for comparison.

Right now

The live figure pools the last three minutes of readings for each route and averages local, limited and Select Bus Service routes, each counting once. “Typical” is the same hour and day type in the latest comparable week.

Week by week

A collector records the whole fleet in short bursts at 9 and 39 minutes past every hour from 6 a.m. to 11 p.m. Eastern. Weekly figures count each local, limited and Select Bus Service route once and give every hour equal weight, with weekdays and weekends kept apart. A week is comparable only when all seven days are in and every hour of the day was caught on at least three weekdays and one weekend day. Up to two hours a week may be missed for each day type; a missed hour is assumed to have moved like the rest of that week’s day relative to its usual level, which leaves a fully caught week’s figure exactly as it is. Express routes and temporary shuttles are left out of the index. Major holidays, and weekdays when far fewer routes ran than usual, are left out of the weekday figures.

Checked against the MTA

Built the same way, the tracker’s figures line up with the MTA’s published hourly route speeds (data.ny.gov r6db-kkzj) route by route. The comparison, refreshed weekly, is on Round and Round.

What this page no longer shows

Earlier versions showed bunching, big gaps and an estimated rider wait. An audit in September 2026 found those numbers could not be defended, and they have been removed while a replacement wait measure is built.

General caveats

  • Positions are GPS readings relayed by the MTA; the feed occasionally drops a bus or reports a stale position.
  • Routes are grouped into boroughs by the letters in their names. Express routes (BM, BxM, QM, SIM, X) form their own group.
  • The full method and every change to it are in the data dictionary and changelog under Download the data.

Counters and fare taps are both partial counts. Taps miss riders who don’t pay: the MTA estimated that 49.6% of local bus riders skipped the fare in the second quarter of 2026 (data.ny.gov uv5h-dfhp). Counters miss riders too: not every bus carries a working one, and the MTA publishes their totals as estimates. Yet systemwide the counters ran only 6% to 13% above taps from June to August 2026, and below taps on more than half of routes in August, so neither is a full count. The ratio between them also drifts by route: on the M15+ it fell from 1.26 counter boardings per tap in July 2025 to 0.99 in July 2026, so a route’s counter trend can move for reasons that have nothing to do with riders. If buses go fare-free, taps stop and the counters are the only route-level count left, which is why the ratio is tracked here now. Each change compares the latest three months two ways: with the same three months a year earlier, which is fair to the season, and with the twelve months before, which shows the recent run. “Route changed” marks a route redrawn by the Queens bus network redesign (June 29 and Aug. 31, 2025) inside that window.
Geography cuts
Bus priority corridors: routes whose shape runs at least 30% of its length within about 60 meters of a marked bus lane or busway (New York City Department of Transportation bus lane data), against routes with little or none (10% or less). Congestion zone: routes the MTA lists as operating in or crossing the Central Business District, against the rest. Each figure is the plain mean of member routes’ weekday speed with stops. Compare each group with its own past: bus lanes go where traffic is worst, so a gap between groups says more about geography than about the lanes.

Data collection — how it runs and what to be skeptical of

How collection runs. One long-running GitHub Actions job polls the MTA BusTime feed about a dozen times over six minutes at 9 and 39 minutes past every hour, 6 a.m. to 11 p.m. Eastern, and saves every bus in service. Before its six-hour time limit it starts its own successor. Until October 2026 each burst was a separately scheduled run, and GitHub skipped many of them; from Oct. 1 to Oct. 6, 2026, only three to five bursts a day were caught. The bar strip at the top of this tray shows the last three weeks, and the daily processor fails loudly when a day comes in under 12 of 17 hours. Nothing is interpolated.

How speed is computed (collector/process.js and collector/rollup.js, method version 2). See the methodology note in the sidebar and the data dictionary. Every day since April 22, 2026, was reprocessed from the raw snapshots when the method changed, so the whole series is computed one way.

Known limits.

  • Straight-line distance over half a minute slightly undercounts a bus turning a corner.
  • Collection caught only some hours of most days before late June 2026, so few spring weeks are comparable.
  • Staten Island express routes read slower than the MTA’s figures, for reasons not yet known. Express routes are kept out of the index.
  • Scheduled trips come from the MTA’s published timetable (GTFS) and exist from Sept. 6, 2026. Across September 2026 the tracker saw 96% to 98% as many buses on the road as trips scheduled to be under way; a trip not run, or a bus whose GPS isn’t reporting, pulls that down.
