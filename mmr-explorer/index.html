New York City  /  Mayor's Management Report  

Report card

The city charter makes New York City grade its own agencies every September. The Mayor's Management Report is that grade — thousands of counts, rates, response times and backlogs, published as a stack of PDFs and a 768,409-row spreadsheet almost nobody opens. This reads the whole thing at once: what moved the way the city says it should, what moved against it, what swung further than it ever has, and what quietly stopped being counted.

Where every number comes from

Two places, and the site always says which. Fiscal 2016 through 2025 come from the city's open data table of agency performance indicators, 768,409 rows, one per indicator per month. Fiscal 2026 comes out of the printed 544-page report, because the data portal has not been refreshed past March 2026. Spending and headcount come from the report's own agency resources tables. Every indicator links to its own raw rows and to the agency chapter it was printed in, so any figure here can be checked against the source in two clicks.

What was actually done to the data

Four things, none of them clever. The full-year figure for an indicator is the row dated 1 June, because the published values are fiscal-year-to-date and June is the last month. Elapsed times are decoded as clock readings only where the title names a colon pair, after testing all 8,245 published time values showed a single blanket rule would have been wrong 74 times. Fiscal 2026 rows were located by requiring each printed row's own 2022–25 columns to equal that indicator's figures in the dataset — never by name, because four rows in the education chapter print as “– Math (%)”. And an indicator counts as having improved only against the direction the city itself says it wants.

What was left alone

No figure is corrected, re-based, smoothed or modelled. Two published values cannot be what their labels say — a percentage filed as 184,112, a thousands column carrying a raw count — and both are flagged in place and left exactly as filed. Forty-six series that the printed report has restated since the data release are shown with both versions of the history. Rankings hold back moves off a near-zero base and one-year moves of 100 per cent or more, and a control on the page puts them straight back in.

What was checked, and how

  • All 2,029 fiscal 2026 matches were re-read from the stored printed line and compared against the dataset again, across 7,960 individual year-cells. No disagreements.
  • Headline figures were checked by hand against the published chapters: felonious assault (29,996), Fire Department end-to-end medical response by fire companies (10:41) and by ambulance (13:09).
  • Twenty randomly drawn matches were read side by side against the printed line in full.
  • Every case where two indicators share an identical history was examined individually. One pair remains genuinely indistinguishable and carries the same figure either way.
  • The eight ratios were built by hand and each checked against the city's own description of both components. An automatic search was run first and thrown away: it returned 4,063 candidates, led by sewer miles divided by satisfaction surveys returned.

What it still cannot tell you

  • Fiscal 2026 rests on a machine reading a PDF. The method refuses rather than guesses, and every 2026 figure shows exactly as printed with a link to its page — but check anything you intend to quote.
  • Roughly 350 printed rows went unplaced. Where that happened the site says an indicator has no fiscal 2026 figure, which is not the same as saying it fell.
  • Spending and headcount stop at fiscal 2025. Those tables have not been refreshed.
  • The history starts at fiscal 2016, where this dataset starts. Dollars are as published and not adjusted for inflation.
  • About three quarters of indicators have no published target, and 1,271 carry no direction the city is willing to name, so they are never scored in either column.

Rebuilding it takes four scripts and no dependencies beyond Python and a PDF reader. Nothing is cached and nothing is hand-edited, so rerunning it after the portal catches up will take fiscal 2026 from the data rather than the PDF. Read the full method, or the code.

Read the fine print before you quote anything. Every figure here is the city's own, carried through without correction, re-basing or modelling. Indicator titles and their descriptions disagree often enough that the description is the only reliable statement of what is being counted — it is shown with every chart. Percentages, counts, dollars and response times are not comparable to one another, and a large percentage change off a small base is usually a change in definition rather than a change on the ground. Where a published figure cannot be what its label says, it is flagged and left exactly as filed.

 

And please read this AI caution.

The concept, the choice of sources and the design direction for this project were set by a human, who pushed hard on verification throughout. The extraction, the code and the checks were done by Anthropic's Claude and Claude Code under that direction.

The quality control that was actually performed is listed under How this was made and in the method: every fiscal 2026 figure located by matching an indicator's own published history rather than its name, all 2,029 matches re-checked across 7,960 year-cells, headline figures verified by hand against the printed chapters, twenty random matches read line by line, and every indistinguishable pair examined individually. The time-encoding rule was rewritten after a test across all 8,245 published values disproved the simpler assumption. Automatically generated ratios were discarded in favour of eight built and checked by hand.

Nevertheless, the risks of using AI remain. If you spot a red flag in anything here, please say so — it is a great deal easier to check one number than four thousand.
