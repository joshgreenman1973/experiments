Method, checks and limits

How this was built

The Mayor's Management Report is required by chapter 1 of the New York City Charter. The city publishes it twice a year: a preliminary edition in January or February covering the first four months of the fiscal year, and the full edition each September covering the fiscal year just ended. It comes out as a book of agency chapters in PDF, and separately as a machine-readable table on the open data portal. This site is built entirely from the machine-readable table, with the PDFs used to check it.

Where the numbers come from

IndicatorsMayor's Management Report — Agency Performance Indicators (rbed-zzin), 768,409 rows: one row per indicator per month, for 6,153 indicators across 53 agencies and citywide initiatives.
Spending and staffMMR Agency Resources (4qmi-txnk) and Preliminary MMR Agency Resources (nvzu-6t9y).
ChaptersThe fiscal 2026 agency chapters, linked from every indicator and every agency page here.
The city's own toolThe Dynamic Mayor's Management Report. This site does not read from it or copy anything out of it — its robots file asks not to be crawled, and that request is respected.

Picking a fiscal year figure out of 768,409 rows

The table carries a row for every indicator for every month, and the value in each row is the figure for the fiscal year so far, not the figure for that month. New York City's fiscal year runs July to June, so the row dated 1 June carries the full-year figure. That is the one used here. It holds regardless of what an indicator's stated reporting period is: indicators reported on a school year or a calendar year still land their annual figure on the June row.

Two checks against the published fiscal 2026 chapters, which print the last five fiscal years side by side:

Police. The chapter's table reads “Felonious assault 25,034 26,959 28,850 29,576 29,996” for fiscal 2022 through 2026. This site reads 28,850 for fiscal 2024 and 29,576 for fiscal 2025.
Fire. The chapter reads “End-to-end average response time to life-threatening medical emergencies by fire companies (minutes:seconds) 9:01 9:24 9:37 9:42 10:41”. This site reads 9:37 for fiscal 2024 and 9:42 for fiscal 2025.

Time figures come in two kinds

The dataset marks 74 of these indicators as measuring elapsed time, and it writes them two incompatible ways without saying which is which in the data itself. The title is the only tell.

  • Where the title names a pair — (minutes:seconds) or (hours:minutes) — the figure is a clock reading. A published 9.42 means nine minutes and forty-two seconds. 44 indicators work this way.
  • Where the title names a single unit — (minutes), (hours), (days) — the figure is an ordinary decimal. A published 74.0 means 74 minutes. 39 of these publish fractions above .59, which could not be a count of seconds, so this is not a guess.
  • Fire Department response times carry no unit in the dataset at all. The published chapter labels every one of them (minutes:seconds), so they are read as clock readings.

Where an indicator is written as clock readings, the drawer shows both the reading and the raw figure as filed, so nothing is taken on trust.

What “moved the city's way” means

The city attaches a desired direction to most indicators — up, down, or none. That label is the city's, not this site's. Scoring here is simply: take an indicator's figure in the baseline year and its figure in the latest complete year, work out the change, and check it against the city's own stated direction.

  • An indicator is scorable only if it has a figure in both years and the city states a direction. Of 2,347 citywide indicators still being reported, about 1,285 clear that bar for the most recent year-on-year comparison.
  • 1,271 indicators carry no desired direction at all. The city counts them and declines to say whether a rise is good news. They are never scored here, in either column.
  • A change smaller than the threshold set on the scoreboard, 1 per cent by default, counts as no material change. You can set that to zero or up to 10 per cent and watch the whole picture move, which is the point of exposing it.
  • Every comparison on the site names both of its years. A change since last year and a change since 2016 are different claims and are never blended.

What this scoring is not: a judgment about whether an agency is doing a good job. It counts how many of an agency's own indicators moved which way. An agency with four indicators and an agency with a hundred are not measured on the same footing, which is why any agency with fewer than ten scorable indicators is left out of the league table, and why the count is always shown next to the share.

The outlier measures

These disagree with each other on purpose. An indicator can be the year's biggest percentage move and completely ordinary against its own record; another can barely move and still be the worst reading the city has ever published.

Change since the year beforePercentage change between an indicator's two most recent published years.
Sustained trend, per yearThe first-to-last change compounded over the years between, so a 60 per cent rise across nine years reads as the 5.4 per cent a year it is. Uses only the two ends of the run.
Trend through every yearA straight line fitted through every published year, as a percentage of the indicator's own average, per year. The fit figure is the share of the movement that line explains: near 1 is a steady march, near 0 means two stray years are doing the work and the rate should not be quoted.
Change across its whole runFirst published year to latest, whatever the gap.
Point changeThe raw difference in the indicator's own units. For a percentage this is percentage points, usually the fairer reading of a rate.
Distance from its own normalHow far the latest figure sits from the average of every earlier year, in standard deviations of that indicator's own history. Finds figures that are strange for themselves rather than merely large.
Best or worst on recordThe latest figure is the highest or lowest in everything published since fiscal 2016.
How much it swingsSpread relative to its own average. Left blank for series that sit at zero in most years, where the ratio means nothing.
Consecutive years one wayAn unbroken run ending at the latest figure. A long run is a harder fact than a single year's move.
Biggest single-year stepThe largest one-year jump anywhere in the series. Above about 100 per cent this is usually a redefinition, which is exactly why it is worth finding.

What gets held back, and what gets flagged

Two different things, and neither of them changes a number.

Held back

Rankings hold back two kinds of move by default, and a control on the page puts them straight back in. A move measured off a near-zero base — where last year's figure was under 1, or under a twentieth of the indicator's own typical value — and any one-year move of 100 per cent or more. Neither proves anything on its own. Between them they account for almost every four-figure percentage in this data, and almost all of those turn out to be a change in how something is counted rather than a change on the ground.

Flagged

A handful of published figures cannot be what their label says. They are shown exactly as the city filed them, marked in the charts and tables, and listed under the outliers tab.

  • The Police Department's fiscal 2020 figure for the share of loud-music noise complaints meeting its response target is filed as 184112, in a series that otherwise runs 83 to 98.
  • 311's fiscal 2022 figure for calls in languages other than English or Spanish is filed as 168355 in a series reported in thousands, where every other year runs 60 to 264. It appears to be that year's raw call count dropped into a column measured in thousands.

A percentage above 100 is not flagged. Plenty of these indicators measure performance against a target — pruning goal completed, revenue as a share of target — and beating the target is the point.

What this cannot tell you

  • The fiscal 2026 report is out; the open data is not. The city published the fiscal 2026 Mayor's Management Report in September 2026, but the underlying dataset was last updated on 12 May 2026 and carries fiscal 2026 only through March. Every full-year figure on this site therefore stops at fiscal 2025. The month-by-month charts show fiscal 2026 as far as it goes and say so.
  • The history starts at fiscal 2016. That is where this dataset starts. The city publishes a separate table for fiscal 2003 to 2012, with no indicator identifiers and no overlap, so joining the two would mean matching on indicator titles across a three-year gap. That is a guess dressed as a series, and it is not attempted here.
  • There are no targets. The printed chapters carry target figures for some indicators. The open dataset does not, so nothing here says whether an agency hit its own target — only which way the number moved.
  • Indicators are not comparable to one another. A response time, a completion rate, a count of summonses and a dollar total share nothing but a row in the same table. The description the city wrote for each one is shown with every chart, because titles and descriptions disagree often enough that the title alone is not safe to quote.
  • Geographic breakouts are the city's own. Where an indicator is broken out by borough, community district, police precinct or school district, the city files each area as its own indicator hanging off a citywide parent. Those 3,274 series are kept out of search and the scoreboard, and appear under the indicator they belong to.
  • Dollars are as published. Spending figures are not adjusted for inflation.

Rebuilding it

Two scripts, no dependencies beyond Python 3. build/fetch.py pages the three tables off the open data portal into build/raw/ and fails loudly if a page comes back short. build/transform.py turns them into the files the page reads. build/stats.py holds the outlier measures, one function, commented with why each one exists. Nothing is cached between runs and nothing is hand-edited, so rerunning it after the city posts fiscal 2026 will pick that year up on its own.
