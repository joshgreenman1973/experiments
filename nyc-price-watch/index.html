Everyday New York prices, each pulled from a named public source and refreshed monthly. Read the full run from May 2024, or switch to 2026 alone; sort by what is rising, falling or still unknown. Nothing here is modeled or filled in — where a number cannot yet be established honestly, the card says so.

Four rules, applied to every item:
  • Real or nothing. Every printed level ties to a named public source with a retrieval date. Points labeled "est." are back-filled from published archives — flagged so you can tell real observations from the back-fill.
  • Same monthly frame. One shared monthly timeline on every card, so any two series can be read against each other. Gaps stay as gaps.
  • One estimator per line. A series carries one source, one method and one geography throughout. We do not join a season average to a single week, or one agency's back-history to another's current readings — that manufactures moves that never happened.
  • Three readings before a line. Two points are not a trend, so sparse series are drawn as separate observations with the count shown.
  • Same spec each month. Same tariff, same panel, same collection rule. No silent sample changes.
  • Work shown. Each month's values and their source notes live in readings.json in the repo.

Trackability label on every card: CLEAN = one published number, comparable by construction · CAVEAT = comparable only if you hold one assumption fixed (we say which) · HARD = not trackable without a deliberate survey method, which the card spells out.

Geographic scope chip on every card: New York City = the five boroughs or a city agency · NYC metro area = the broader region (includes parts of New Jersey) · U.S. city average = a national figure, not New York-specific. Four lines (gas, both CPI series, Case-Shiller) are metro, not city; one (the BEC ingredient index) is national.

Can every line be tracked monthly?

Yes — the chart is now on a monthly frame. The data cadence supports monthly for nearly all of them; the line densifies as real readings accumulate in readings.json. Three honest limits remain.

Monthly, no problemMonthly but with a catchMonthly only with legwork
Gas (AAA daily), ConEd electric & gas, StreetEasy rent, all five borough rents (StreetEasy, one consistent method), grocery & restaurant CPI, the BEC ingredient index (BLS average prices), Broadway (weekly grosses → monthly), and the five rare-change tariffs (verify monthly). Case-Shiller is monthly but always ~2 months lagged — the lag is constant, so it stays comparable. Family dinner is monthly only if the 500-restaurant panel is re-run that often (heavy); quarterly is the realistic cap. Pizza, bagel, coffee, halal cart, bacon-egg-and-cheese, Uber. The data exists nowhere public — monthly requires a fixed in-person survey route (and a phone for Uber). Doable monthly, but it's the one place the limit is human effort, not data.

Bottom line: most lines can be truly monthly today; the seven survey/quote items can be monthly too, but only if someone walks the route. None are stuck at a coarser cadence for data reasons except the deliberately heavy family-dinner panel.

How the monthly line is drawn

Step-function rates (subway, toll, Citi Bike, water, taxi) are filled for every month at the rate then in effect — these lines are real, not estimates. Published series (gas, both CPI lines, StreetEasy rents, Case-Shiller, ConEd bills) plot the actual monthly readings we have; the line connects them and fills in as new months arrive. Broadway holds the season average across its months with the latest weekly read at the end. Survey items stay dashed until the first in-person collection.

Each dot is an actual observation; the red dot is the latest. On est. series the line between two known readings is a straight interpolation — it shows direction, not a month-exact value — so the real, dated points are the dots, and the segments connecting them fill in with real readings as the months accumulate.

Back-fill provenance

Pre-2026 points on the verified cards come from these archives: MTA fare and toll press releases, AAA / FRED monthly averages (series APUS12A7471A), NYC Water Board annual rate schedules, ConEd residential rate history, Broadway League season statistics, NYC TLC tariff press releases, and StreetEasy back-calculated from year-over-year change. Each point's source note is in readings.json. Gas, ConEd and the back-calculated rents are approximate between known points — the shape is right, the month-to-month precision is not. Marked est. where that applies.

The income side

Costs only tell half the affordability story, so two wage gauges sit in a green income group: the QCEW average weekly wage (the comprehensive UI-records figure, bonus-inclusive and lagged) and the Employment Cost Index (the cleanest pay-growth signal, which holds the job mix constant). Both are averages — useful for direction, but they don't capture the typical worker as well as a median would.

What this doesn't measure

A true wages-minus-costs burden, median (vs. average) pay, tip inflation, shrinkflation, rent-stabilized contracts, or the full cost of owning a car. Directional gauge, not a cost-of-living index.
