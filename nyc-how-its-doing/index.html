How New York Is Doing

Of the 38 measures with a direction and enough history to judge, 14 were better in their latest year than their average over the five years before, 13 were worse and 11 showed no clear change.

This edition tracks 48 measures of life in New York City in 11 areas, from jobs and rents to overdoses, test scores and bus speeds. 26 of them can be compared with the other largest U.S. cities on the same definition, and New York’s rank is shown for each. A fixed rule, published on the methods page, decides what counts as better or worse; no one picks the headlines. This edition measures conditions in the city. How New Yorkers feel about them needs a survey of residents, which this edition does not have.

What this edition can’t measure

  • Poverty rate counting New York costs: Values exist for 2015-2024 only and 2025 is not yet published. The publisher warns that pre-2020 rates are not directly comparable with 2020 onward (a sampling change), so the five-year comparison straddles a break in the series. Values are whole percentages read from the report's chart labels; no table or margin of error is published.
  • Licensed child care seats: Only a current snapshot is published (active programs as of 2026-11-15), so there is no annual history to compare against. 360 of 2746 programs (13%) list no capacity and are left out of the total.
  • Average bus speed: Only 7 full years (2019-2025) are available, fewer than the 5-year baseline plus 10-year swing the report uses.
  • Streets rated acceptably clean: The indicator ended after fiscal 2023: the fiscal 2023 Mayor's Management Report (MMR) removed it while the Scorecard inspection program is overhauled, and no fiscal 2024-fiscal 2026 values exist. fiscal 2019 has no rating (MMR prints NA).
  • Adults who volunteered: The survey is every two years and the consistent 2017-2023 analytic file gives only 4 points (2017, 2019, 2021, 2023); the 2025 wave is not released yet. Too short for the five-year baseline and ten-year swing.
  • How residents feel about the city, their neighborhoods, their safety and their plans to stay: New York has no regular representative survey of residents.
  • Noise exposure: only complaints to 311 exist, not measurements.
  • Homelessness in one daily number: the shelter census covers the Department of Homeless Services only.
  • Neighborhood results for each of the 59 community districts: planned for a later edition.
