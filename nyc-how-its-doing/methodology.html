Methods, sources and caveats

How a measure is scored

Each measure is reduced to one value per year for New York City. The latest year is compared with the average of the years in the five years before it. If fewer than two years fall in that window (some series are published every two or three years), the two most recent earlier values within ten years are used instead.

To judge whether a change is large, the change is divided by the typical year-to-year move in that measure: the median of the absolute changes between consecutive values in the ten years before the latest year. A change smaller than one typical move is reported as no clear change. So is any change smaller than the published margin of error of the latest value, where one exists (Census survey figures).

Every measure has a direction set before the data was gathered: up is better (jobs, incomes, graduation), down is better (poverty, overdoses, crime), or none (population, enrollment, jail population), in which case the page says higher or lower. The list of measures and their directions is in the project’s registry file and changes only with an entry in the changelog.

The opening chart places each measure by that ratio, flipped where needed so better is always to the right. The lists are ordered by its size. No one chooses which results lead.

The five years before 2025 include the pandemic, so a five-year average can make a return to normal look like improvement or decline. Wherever a measure has a 2019 value, the page shows it beside the average. The scoring rule itself does not use it.

When a source changed its definition partway through a series, the measure is scored only from the change on, and the page says so: Students in grades 3 to 8 proficient in reading, from 2023, because the state changed the tests and the cut scores for proficiency in 2023; Students in grades 3 to 8 proficient in math, from 2023, because the state changed the tests and the cut scores for proficiency in 2023; Poverty rate counting New York costs, from 2020, because the Poverty Tracker says figures before 2020 are not comparable with later ones; Trust for Public Land ParkScore, from 2020, because the Trust for Public Land overhauled its scoring in 2019 and added an equity measure in 2020; City debt per resident, from fiscal 2022, because a 2022 accounting rule moved leases onto the books, raising reported debt. When a publisher warns against comparing its figures from year to year, the measure is shown but not scored: State and local taxes on a typical family, because the report that publishes it advises against comparing its estimates from year to year.

Peer comparisons use the 25 largest U.S. cities by the Census Bureau’s July 2025 population estimates: New York, Los Angeles, Chicago, Houston, Phoenix, Philadelphia, San Antonio, San Diego, Dallas, Fort Worth, Jacksonville, Austin, San Jose, Charlotte, Columbus, Indianapolis, San Francisco, Seattle, Denver, Nashville-Davidson, Oklahoma City, Washington, D.C., El Paso, Las Vegas, Boston. Los Angeles, Chicago, Houston and Philadelphia are shown by name throughout. Where a source reports counties, metro areas, school districts or transit agencies rather than cities, the measure says so.

Every number on the site is written by the build script from the data files. After building, the script checks every number in the page text against the list of values it printed and stops if it finds one it can’t account for.

  • Covers jobs located in the city, whoever holds them; government workers are excluded.
  • Peers left out: Current Employment Statistics (CES) earnings series at the city level exist for New York City but not for most peer cities.

Method
