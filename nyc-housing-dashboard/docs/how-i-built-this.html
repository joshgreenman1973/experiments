How I built this

Every number on the page comes from five public sources, combined by two scripts that stop if their checks fail.

The sources

  • Housing Database, project-level file, version 26Q2.1, from the Department of City Planning. One row per Department of Buildings job since 2010 that adds or removes homes: new buildings, conversions and demolitions, with the net change in permanent homes (the city calls them Class A units), the year the job was permitted and the year it was finished. 111,909 residential jobs. It runs through June 30, 2026.
  • City Planning's own summaries of the same database by 2024 Council district and community district, used as a check on the project-level sums and for each district's 2020 count of homes.
  • Affordable Housing Production by Building, from the Department of Housing Preservation and Development (HPD): 9,551 buildings in projects started from Jan. 1, 2014 through June 30, 2026, with homes by income tier.
  • The draft Fair Housing Growth Strategy, released Aug. 19, 2026 by HPD and City Planning under Local Law 167 of 2023. Pages 36 to 42 give a five-year target for each of the 59 community districts. I read the tables out of the PDF with a script and saved them as a CSV. They add up to 350,260, against the report's citywide target of 350,000; the difference is the report's own rounding.
  • The Census Bureau: 2020 census block populations (from the bureau's TIGERweb map service) and the American Community Survey five-year estimates for 2020 to 2024, by census tract: household income brackets, gross rent, rent asked for vacant units, rent as a share of income and tenure.
  • District boundaries from City Planning, clipped to the shoreline. Council members from the Council's district list, read Oct. 22, 2026. HPD's 2026 area median income chart.

The build, step by step

  1. scripts/fetch.py downloads everything. It refuses to save a file that comes back short: fewer than 100,000 Housing Database jobs, 9,000 HPD buildings, 51 or 59 districts, or a block population under 8 million.
  2. scripts/build.py places each 2020 census block in a Council district and a community district by the block's internal point. Block populations add up to 8,804,190, the 2020 census count for the city, exactly; the build stops otherwise.
  3. It sums the Housing Database jobs into districts by year, using the district each job already carries in City Planning's file.
  4. It sums HPD buildings into districts using the district HPD assigns each building.
  5. It splits each census tract's survey numbers among districts in proportion to where the tract's 2020 residents live, then estimates district medians from the summed brackets.
  6. The page does the rest in the browser: year ranges, per-person rates, rankings and the counts written into sentences change with the controls.

Net homes and districts

A "net home" is the change in permanent homes from one Buildings Department job: a new 100-unit building on an empty lot adds 100; a demolished two-family house subtracts two; turning an office into 40 apartments adds 40. Completed homes are counted in the year City Planning records the job as finished. Permitted homes are counted in the year the job got its construction permit, for jobs that are permitted, partly finished or finished; jobs withdrawn after a permit are left out, so a permitted year can shrink a little as the file is updated.

Seven jobs have no Council district and 43 have no community district (36 of those sit in parks or airports, which are not community districts). They count toward citywide totals but no district.

Calculated figures

  • Shares such as "the 10 districts that built the most account for 51%" divide the sum for those districts by the citywide sum for the same years.
  • "Districts that account for half" counts districts from the top until their sum reaches half of the citywide total.
  • Rates per 1,000 residents divide by the 2020 census population and by the number of years selected. 2026 counts as half a year, since the data stop on June 30.
  • The citywide totals are my sums of the project file. They differ from City Planning's own summary tables by at most 580 homes in any district over 2010 to June 2026, 580 of it in 2017, when City Planning's summary counts more completions than the project file does, and 208 in the first half of 2026, all in Queens Community District 7.

The targets chart

Target pace is the district's five-year goal divided by five. Past pace is net homes completed from 2016 through 2025, divided by 10. I picked 2016 to 2025 because that is the window the city used to sort districts into growth cohorts (page 29 of the draft). For the income-restricted view, past pace is HPD's new construction in projects started 2016 to 2025, divided by 10; HPD counts a project when financing closes, so these homes are counted earlier than the completions in the all-homes view.

Share of homes completed that were income-restricted

The Housing Database records, for each completed job, how many of its homes HPD counts as income-restricted. The share divides that count by the homes added in completed jobs over the selected years (gross additions, before subtracting demolitions, since the restricted homes are among the additions). The pipeline view in the first section counts homes in every job with a construction permit that City Planning has not recorded as finished; it is not tied to a year, and some of those jobs have stalled. The growth view divides net homes by the 2020 census count of housing units in the district, which matches how the city measured past growth when it set the targets.

Median income

The Census Bureau does not publish figures for Council or community districts. I added up each tract's household counts in 16 income brackets, split by where the tract's residents live, and found the median by straight-line interpolation inside the bracket where the middle household falls. When the middle household falls in the top bracket, which has no upper edge ($200,000 and up for income, $3,500 and up for rent), the page reports that floor with "or more"; this happens for income in Manhattan Community District 1 and for rent in Manhattan Districts 1 and 5. Run on the whole city, this gives $80,890; the bureau's published 2020-2024 median for New York City is $80,483, half a percent lower. The target pace line in the scatter chart is 70,000 homes a year divided by the 2020 population: 8.0 per 1,000 residents.

Rents

Affordable means a rent of 30 percent of income or less. I used a two-person household: 60 percent of HPD's 2026 median income for two people is $81,420 a year, or $2,036 a month; 100 percent is $135,700, or $3,393 a month. Shares are interpolated inside the census rent brackets. Gross rent includes utilities; the asking rent for vacant units is contract rent, without them, so the vacant view is a slightly easier test. A district would be left blank in the vacant view if the survey found fewer than 150 vacant units for rent there, after weighting; none fell under that line in this release.

Use net homes, with demolitions subtracted

Decision
Every count is net of homes lost.
Why
The Chicago dashboard this follows could not subtract demolitions; New York's Housing Database can. A district that tears down two-family houses for a slightly larger building should not get credit for the gross count.
Effect
Jobs completed 2016 to 2025 added 315,320 homes and removed 21,586; the page shows the difference, 293,734.
Who
Claude.

Show both Council districts and community districts

Decision
A switch at the top changes every chart except the targets, which exist only for community districts.
Why
The Council votes on rezonings, and by custom it follows the local member on projects in that member's district; the city's targets are set for community districts.
Who
Claude.

Name the current Council member

Decision
Each Council district shows who represents it now, with a note in the district panel that most homes finished in a given year were approved years earlier, often under someone else.
Alternative
Leave names off, or match each year to the member then serving. Matching by year would still misassign credit, since approvals happen years before completion.
Who
Claude.

Replace hand-collected listings with census rents

Decision
The Chicago dashboard checked two snapshots of listings, taken in late July. I used the census survey: rents tenants pay, and asking rents for vacant units, for 2020 to 2024.
Why
It covers every district with a published method.
Cost
Five-year survey numbers trail the market and understate current rents, and the vacant-unit sample is small.
Who
Claude.

Count HPD homes by project start

Decision
Income-restricted homes are dated by when the project started, the date HPD reports against its own goals.
Effect
These are homes financed, not finished. Many started since 2023 are still under construction.
Who
Claude, following HPD's convention.

Split census tracts by where people live

Decision
A tract that straddles a district line is split by its 2020 block populations.
Alternative
Assign each tract wholly to one district, or split by land area. Both move more people into the wrong district.
Who
Claude.

What this does not do

  • It does not count basement apartments and other homes created without a Buildings Department job, or homes built illegally.
  • The income-restricted counts are what HPD counts toward the city's housing plans. They are not a count of all income-restricted or public housing in the city.
  • It does not say why a district built what it did. Zoning, land prices, transit and Council decisions all play a part; the page shows outcomes.
  • The targets are a draft. The final report is due later in 2026, and the numbers may change.

Checks run

  • 2020 block populations sum to 8,804,190, the census count, exactly. 1,760 residents fall outside every Council district line and 3,604 outside every community district, along the shore and in the parks and airports that are not community districts.
  • District completions match City Planning's own summaries within 580 homes over 16 and a half years.
  • The 2020 housing units in City Planning's district tables sum to 3,618,635, the census count for the city.
  • HPD's income tiers add up to its counted homes in every building; the build stops on any mismatch.
  • Every HPD building has a Council district and a community district; the build stops if one does not.
  • The citywide median income from summed brackets lands within 0.5 percent of the published figure.
  • The 59 targets sum to within 260 of the report's 350,000.
