Where you can sit down

Across 4,899 classified street seating locations installed by the New York City Department of Transportation since 2011, backed benches make up less than half (2,160, or 44.1%) — matched one-for-one by backless benches (2,160) plus 558 leaning bars — and in 2023–2026, 76.7% of classified installations had no backrest at all.

Three city inventories record where a person can sit outside along New York City streets and plazas without buying anything: the Department of Transportation (DOT) Seating Locations dataset (esmy-s8q5, 5,776 sidewalk and bus-stop installations), the Department of City Planning (DCP) Privately Owned Public Spaces database (rvih-nhyn, 392 sites, of which 217 explicitly require public seating or tables) and DOT’s 90 Pedestrian Plazas (5dck-9m6g). (The Department of Parks and Recreation does not publish a citywide park-bench point inventory on NYC Open Data, so interior park benches are outside these street counts.) Together, the three street-level datasets show two distinct patterns: a sharp shift by DOT away from backed benches toward backless benches and standing-only leaning bars, and wide gaps across outer-borough residential neighborhoods where more than half of all land is farther than a five-minute walk from any mapped public street seat.

2. The borough divide and residential bench deserts

When leaning bars are excluded, New York City has 5,525 mapped street and plaza seating sites: 5,218 DOT benches (2,160 backed, 2,160 backless, 21 unknown bench and 877 unclassified 2023–2026 installations), 217 POPS with required seating and 90 DOT pedestrian plazas. Because 208 of the 217 seating POPS and 35 of the 90 pedestrian plazas are in Manhattan, only 6.0% of residential land in Manhattan is more than a five-minute street walk (0.25 miles) from a mapped public seat. In Queens, 55.0% of residential land is beyond a five-minute walk; in Staten Island, 76.6% is beyond five minutes and 48.9% is beyond ten minutes.

Even where overall bench counts look high, the type of seat varies sharply by borough. The Bronx has the highest per-capita count of total public seat sites (9.40 per 10,000 residents), driven by a rapid 2023–2026 rollout, yet it has more backless benches (581) and leaning bars (172) than backed benches (380) among its classified installations.

How this was done

Primary sources and record counts. Three municipal datasets were downloaded from NYC Open Data on Nov. 6, 2026: (1) Department of Transportation (DOT) Seating Locations (esmy-s8q5), containing 5,776 unique siteid records with valid coordinates; (2) Department of City Planning (DCP) Privately Owned Public Spaces (POPS) (rvih-nhyn), containing 392 records, of which 217 explicitly list Seating or Tables in amenities_required (and 168 of those 217 require 24-hour public access); and (3) DOT Pedestrian Plazas (5dck-9m6g), containing 90 polygon records represented by their geometric centroids.

Classification of DOT street seating. In esmy-s8q5, 4,899 of the 5,776 rows carry an asset_subtype and an installation_date: BACKLESS 2.0 (1,636), BACKED 1.0 (1,194), BACKED 2.0 (938), LEANING BAR (558), BACKLESS 1.0 (524), WORLDS FAIR (28, backed wood-slat benches) and UNKNOWN BENCH (21). The remaining 877 records carry site IDs from SEAT.2023.* (1), SEAT.2024.* (472), SEAT.2025.* (355) and SEAT.2026.* (49) with blank asset_subtype, asset_id and installation_date fields. Those 877 records are included in total DOT bench counts and nearest-seat lookups and are broken out separately in the annual chart and borough table rather than imputed to a subtype.

Walk-distance and neighborhood calculations. Every point was spatially joined using shapely to shoreline-clipped 2020 Neighborhood Tabulation Areas (nyc_nta_2020.geojson, 262 polygons) and matched to 2020 Decennial Census population counts (populations.json). To measure walk access across each neighborhood’s land area rather than just at its centroid, a regular grid of land points (~250-meter spacing) was sampled inside every shoreline-clipped NTA polygon. At each sample point, straight-line haversine distance to the nearest public seat site (DOT bench excluding leaning bars, POPS with required seating or DOT pedestrian plaza) was multiplied by a 1.25× Manhattan-grid street routing factor and converted to walking minutes at 3.0 mph (20 minutes per mile). Neighborhood rankings sort on unrounded per-capita rates before display formatting.

What is not in the data. The New York City Department of Parks and Recreation publishes a directory of park properties and Comfort Stations on NYC Open Data, but does not publish a citywide point inventory of interior park benches (Central Park, Prospect Park and other conservancies maintain separate donor-plaque maps). Consequently, all figures on this page measure street, sidewalk, plaza and POPS seating outside park interiors.
