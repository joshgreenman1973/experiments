What if NYC charged for street parking?

New York City has roughly 3 million curb-side parking spaces. About 85,000 — just 2.8% — are metered, generating roughly $258 million a year. The rest are free. Adjust the sliders below to model what different pricing structures could raise.

Economist Donald Shoup has argued that free curb parking generates excess traffic, raises the cost of housing and that the market value of the land devoted to free parking in the U.S. may exceed the value of all the cars parked on it. At the same time, car owners in New York City value free parking precisely because owning a car here is already expensive and often inconvenient — and many people, including families, residents with mobility issues and workers with difficult commutes or odd-hour shifts, genuinely depend on private vehicles.

Free overnight street parking in New York City dates to 1954, when the city began allowing drivers to store cars on public streets at no charge. At the time only 43% of city households owned a car, and those households had above-average incomes. Before 1954 the New York Times had proposed an annual parking tax of $60 — roughly $550 in today's dollars.

Two kinds of curb space, two kinds of pricing

Not all 3 million curb spaces are alike. Metered zones make sense for commercial corridors, transit hubs and high-traffic streets — the kinds of blocks where drivers circle for spots and turnover matters. Today's ~85,000 meters are concentrated in Manhattan below 96th Street, downtown Brooklyn, Long Island City and commercial strips in the Bronx and Staten Island. Expanding metering to all major commercial and mixed-use streets citywide could cover roughly 300,000 to 500,000 spaces.

Residential permits target a different problem: the vast majority of curb space sits on neighborhood streets, occupied overnight and on weekends by local residents. These spaces — estimated at 1.5 million to 2.5 million — are not candidates for hourly meters, but a modest annual permit fee could generate revenue and deter commuters from storing cars in residential neighborhoods. The two policies are not mutually exclusive: a city could meter commercial streets and charge residential permits at the same time. The first four cards below model each approach independently; the fifth shows what combining metering with residential permits could raise, and the sixth tests a permit program with senior- and family-exempt tiers.

Methodology and sources

This calculator is a rough modeling tool, not a precise forecast. All figures are estimates.

  • 3 million curb spaces: Widely cited figure confirmed by NYC DOT Commissioner testimony and multiple transportation studies.
  • ~85,000 metered spaces (2.8%): NYC DOT data; the Open Data portal lists ~12,900 active meter records, but each record represents a blockface segment with multiple physical meters. DOT reports ~85,000 individual metered spaces.
  • $258 million current revenue: NYC Comptroller reports on parking meter cash collection, FY2023-2024 estimates.
  • Demand-based model: Based on Transportation Alternatives' EasyPark proposal, which estimated $4.59 billion at ~$2.93/hr average. Formula here: spaces × hours × occupancy × rate × days. Defaults ($2.50/hr, 10 metered hrs, 35% paid occupancy, 300 days) reproduce roughly $7.9B — somewhat above TransAlt because this tool applies metering to all 3M spaces rather than a narrower "metered zones" subset. Lowering hours or occupancy quickly brings the total into the TransAlt range.
  • Auction model: Inspired by City Journal's "The case for auctioning New York City's parking spaces" by Adam Lehodey (March 2026).
  • Equity-tiered permit: Exempts households with a resident 65+ or a child under 6, charges everyone else. Default 30% exempt share is a midpoint between the share of New York City residents 65+ (~16% per ACS) and the share of households with a child under 6 (~8%), adjusted upward because seniors are over-represented among car-owning households. The exempt share is a parker-level estimate, not a household-level one; the slider lets you test other assumptions.
  • Residential permit pricing: Modeled on London's resident parking permits (~$150-225/year) and San Francisco's residential parking permit program.
  • Donald Shoup research: Shoup documented 366,000 excess vehicle miles traveled per year from cruising for parking in just a 15-block Upper West Side study area.
  • Hunter College estimate: Hunter College Urban Policy & Planning, "Shifting Gears: Transitioning to a Car-Light New York City" (2024) estimated that charging market-rate prices for parking at current volume could generate $12.3 billion in annual revenue.
  • Center for an Urban Future: "5 Revenue-Raising Ideas for NYC" identified expanded paid street parking as one of five proposals that could collectively generate over $1.4 billion annually.
  • History of free parking: City Journal, "Parking madness" traces the origins of free overnight parking in NYC to a 1954 policy decision.
  • Street parking map: The green lines show 80,658 street segments that have designated parking lanes, from NYC's Centerline dataset (field: number_park_lanes > 0). These are streets where parking is physically possible — most are unmetered/free, subject to alternate-side and other time restrictions. Not every linear foot is parkable (fire hydrants, crosswalks, driveways reduce usable curb), but the segments indicate where the city has allocated curb space for parking.

Meter location data: NYC Open Data, Parking Meters Locations and Status. Street centerline data: NYC Open Data, Centerline.

What parking revenue could pay for

New York City's $258 million in annual parking meter revenue amounts to 1.3% of the MTA's $19.9 billion operating budget. Even modest pricing of free curb spaces could meaningfully change that equation. Here is where MTA funding comes from now and what it pays for.

Where MTA revenue comes from

The MTA's $19.9 billion operating budget is funded by a mix of fares, dedicated taxes, tolls and government subsidies. Current parking meter revenue ($258 million) is a sliver — but a priced-curb scenario from the calculator could replace one of the larger pieces.

What it costs to run

Fares cover roughly 35% of operating costs, down from more than 50% before the pandemic. The average NYC Transit trip costs about $5.63 to provide; riders pay about $1.97 on average (once unlimited passes and reduced fares are blended in). The rest is subsidy.
