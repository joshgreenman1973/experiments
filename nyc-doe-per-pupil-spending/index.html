New York City public schools: spending, fiscal years 2016 to 2025

Per-pupil K-12 spending in New York City: where the money goes

Among the 100 largest school districts in the United States, New York City spent the most per pupil in fiscal year (FY) 2024, the latest year the U.S. Census Bureau has published. This page shows how that figure is built, how much of its growth comes from inflation and shrinking enrollment and where the money goes. The figures come from the Census Bureau, the New York City Independent Budget Office (IBO), the City Council's Finance Division, the Department of Education (DOE), the New York State Education Department (NYSED), the Bureau of Labor Statistics and the National Center for Education Statistics (NCES). Two sections also carry figures reported by Chalkbeat, boxed and labeled. City fiscal years run July to June, so FY2024 is the 2023-24 school year.

1. The long climb: district schools only, measured the same way each year

One definition throughout: the Census Bureau's current spending per pupil for the New York City School District, from its Annual Survey of School System Finances. Current spending covers day-to-day costs, including salaries, health insurance and pension contributions. Census leaves out charter schools (both their students and the department's payments to them), payments to private schools, capital outlay and debt service. Enrollment is the fall count of students in DOE schools reported to the federal Common Core of Data, which includes some pre-K pupils. Toggle between per-pupil and total district spending, and between dollars as spent and dollars adjusted for inflation.

Use the inflation toggle. It changes the story more than anything else on this page. In dollars as spent, per-pupil spending rose 48.5 percent between FY2016 and FY2024 and the district spending behind it rose 27.9 percent. In constant FY2024 dollars, per-pupil spending rose 18.5 percent and total spending rose 2.1 percent: $29.64 billion in FY2016 against $30.27 billion in FY2024, below the FY2022 peak of $33.62 billion. Split the 48.5 percent rise into its parts and a little under three-fifths is inflation, about two-fifths is the shrinking student count and about one-twentieth is more money after inflation.
The FY2022 spike and the FY2023 dip. Per-pupil spending rose 20 percent in FY2022, to $35,914 from $29,931, as current spending rose 13 percent and enrollment fell 5.9 percent. It fell to $33,387 in FY2023 before returning to $35,796 in FY2024. Both moves are in the published Census series. In constant FY2024 dollars, per-pupil spending peaked at $39,111 in FY2022 and has not returned to that level. IBO's figures show federal aid to the department nearly doubling in FY2022, to $6.1 billion from $3.3 billion (in 2025 dollars), as pandemic relief arrived, then falling; neither source assigns the swing to a single cause. Charter schools are outside this series. The Census survey excludes charter schools whose charters are held by nongovernmental entities, which describes New York's. The y-axis does not start at zero by default, which visually amplifies year-to-year movement; use the toggle to see the zero-baseline version.

2. Why the line goes up: it's a denominator story too

Per-pupil spending is a ratio, and both parts move. Between FY2016 and FY2024, Census-reported district enrollment fell from 981,667 to 845,509 (-13.9 percent). The district spending behind the per-pupil figure rose from $23.67 billion to $30.27 billion in dollars as spent (+27.9 percent), but in constant FY2024 dollars from $29.64 billion to $30.27 billion, a rise of just 2.1 percent. Set against a 13.9 percent enrollment decline, that is most of the story of rising per-pupil spending.

Total district current spending is the Table 18 per-pupil figure times that enrollment, which equals the Census numerator: current spending minus payments to charter and private schools. Constant dollars use the consumer price index for all urban consumers (CPI-U), New York-Newark-Jersey City, averaged over each July-June fiscal year. Once you adjust for inflation, there is little numerator growth left to explain. Real district spending rose 2.1 percent across nine years while real per-pupil spending rose 18.5 percent. The difference is the denominator: fewer students dividing a nearly constant pot. Had enrollment stayed at its FY2016 level, FY2024's spending would have come to $30,831 per pupil instead of $35,796; measured that way, fewer students account for 42 percent of the nominal per-pupil rise (33 percent measured the other way) and about 87 to 89 percent of the real rise. New York City is spending about the same inflation-adjusted amount on its district schools as in FY2016, on substantially fewer children.
Two notes on this comparison. First, the total here is the numerator Census itself divides by enrollment, so per-pupil spending times enrollment reproduces it exactly. Census Table 16 prints a larger "current spending" figure ($34.97 billion in FY2024) because it also includes $4.33 billion in payments to charter and private schools and $0.38 billion for adult education and other programs outside kindergarten through 12th grade. An earlier version of this page used the Table 16 figure and concluded, wrongly, that Census computes per-pupil amounts on an unpublished pupil base; the difference is in the numerator, not the denominator. Second, the inflation adjustment uses the New York-area CPI-U, the same regional index IBO uses. The national CPI-U, which an earlier version of this page used, gives a 14.3 percent real rise in per-pupil spending and a 1.6 percent real decline in total spending. School costs are mostly wages, so a wage-based index could show a different real trend.

3. The charter sector, which the spending data leaves out

The Census series above excludes charter schools entirely. They are separate local education agencies under New York law, but they enroll New York City children and are funded with public money, so leaving them out understates how many students the city's public system serves. Between 2012-13 and 2025-26, charter enrollment in New York City grew from 58,493 to 149,879 (+156 percent), while district enrollment fell from 985,388 to 810,653 (-17.7 percent). The number of charter school locations in the state file rose from 159 to 285. Figures for 2025-26 are from NYSED's preliminary release.

Source: New York State Education Department (NYSED), Basic Educational Data System (BEDS) Day enrollment files, school-level "All Students" tables, each school year's own release. Figures are the sum of pre-K-12 enrollment for all schools located in the five New York City counties (Bronx, Kings, New York, Queens, Richmond), split by NYSED's own field classifying each school as public or charter. District counts include pre-K pupils in district school buildings (about 22,000 to 38,000 a year) but not children at community-based pre-K centers. Counting both sectors changes the size of the decline, but does not explain it. From 2012-13 to 2025-26 the district lost 174,735 students while charters gained 91,386. Adding the two together, total public enrollment in New York City fell 8.0 percent rather than the 17.7 percent the district figure alone shows. That is an arithmetic point about which students the Census series counts, not a finding that charter growth caused the district decline.
Why this chart cannot tell you where students went. The two lines are counts, not flows. A child enrolling in a charter kindergarten was never in a district school to begin with, so charter growth is not the same as students transferring out of the district. District enrollment also fell for reasons this chart does not measure at all: fewer births in the city, families moving out and shifts to private schooling or homeschooling. No source on this page apportions the district decline among those causes, and this page does not attempt to.

Comparability caveats. These NYSED counts are BEDS Day (fall snapshot) enrollment and do not match the Census enrollment used in the charts above, which comes from the federal Common Core of Data. For the same school year, the Census district count is higher by between about 1,500 and 22,500 students, depending on the year. NYSED school years are labeled by span (2025-26); Census fiscal years are labeled by end year. This chart shows enrollment only. It does not show charter per-pupil spending, which comes from a different funding stream and is not reported in the Census district series or in the IBO breakdowns below.

4. Hold harmless: when enrollment falls but the budget doesn't

The two sections above show enrollment falling. This section is about what the city decided to do with school budgets while that happened. Under the city's Fair Student Funding formula, a school's budget follows its students: fewer students should mean a smaller allocation. Every year since FY2021, the city has softened or suspended that consequence for schools that lost students. The policy is called "hold harmless," and it is one reason per-pupil spending has climbed at schools that are shrinking.

How it works, step by step

  1. In spring, every school is given a budget for the coming year based on projected enrollment, run through the Fair Student Funding formula: a foundation amount for each school, plus an amount per student weighted by grade level, with added weights for students who need academic intervention, English language learners, students with disabilities, students in temporary housing and schools with a concentration of high-need students.
  2. In the fall, actual enrollment is counted from each school's register at the end of October.
  3. Normally, mid-year the budget is trued up. Schools that enrolled more students than projected get more money; schools that enrolled fewer have money taken back.
  4. Under hold harmless, the reduction is switched off. A school that projected 400 students and enrolled 340 keeps the budget it was given for 400. Schools that gained students still get their increase; the adjustment only runs one way. In recent years the city has also protected schools from cuts in their spring budgets.
  5. The gap between what the formula says the school should get and what it actually keeps is the hold harmless cost, and it is paid for centrally.
The mechanism matters more than the label. Hold harmless does not add a line to a school's budget marked "hold harmless." It removes a subtraction. That is why a school can be described as receiving millions in hold harmless funding while its principal experiences it simply as the budget not being cut.

What official sources say it costs

DOE's class size plan for 2025-26 states that "New York City has invested a total of $1.2 billion since FY 2021 in 'hold harmless' funding for schools losing enrollment to ensure they can maintain services" (NYC Public Schools, SY2025-2026 Class Size Reduction Plan, July 2025). The City Council Finance Division's tally of DOE's school allocation memos puts the total for FY2021 through FY2026 at $1.64 billion, including $388 million in FY2026 (2025-26): $127 million allocated before the school year and $262 million at mid-year. For FY2027 (2026-27), DOE released $286 million in initial hold harmless allocations in June 2026, according to the city comptroller, bringing the comptroller's count since FY2021 to $1.93 billion; these allocation totals exclude centrally budgeted fringe benefits. When Chancellor Aviles-Ramos announced the 2024-25 mid-year hold harmless in November 2024, DOE said the mid-year adjustment it waived would have totaled $157 million.

Reported figures, not primary-verified

The figures below come from Chalkbeat's June 22, 2026, analysis of Department of Education data, "NYC's $1.9 billion dilemma: How long can schools be 'held harmless' for enrollment losses?" The underlying data is DOE's, but the school counts and school-level figures are Chalkbeat's, and this page has not reproduced them from source files. All refer to the 2026-27 school year.

  • Nearly $290 million for 2026-27, which Chalkbeat says is more than double what the city spent before the 2025-26 school year began (the full 2025-26 total, after the mid-year allocation, was higher, at about $388 million).
  • 723 schools received hold harmless funds for 2026-27; 55 received more than $1 million each.
  • Nearly $1.9 billion cumulatively since 2020, consistent with the comptroller's $1.93 billion.

Two concrete schools:

  • I.S. 339, Bronx. Enrollment fell from 315 students in 2020 to 150 in 2025-26. The school is slated to get nearly $2.5 million in hold harmless funding for 2026-27, roughly a third of its $7.8 million budget. Put differently, about one dollar in three at this school is money the funding formula says it should no longer be getting.
  • Urban Assembly Institute of Math and Science for Young Women, Downtown Brooklyn. Enrollment fell from nearly 500 in 2020 to 350. It is slated to receive about $374,000 for 2026-27, a much smaller cushion because it lost a much smaller share of its students.
Why this belongs on a per-pupil page. Per-pupil spending is spending divided by students. Hold harmless holds the numerator steady at schools where the denominator is falling, which pushes per-pupil spending up at exactly those schools, mechanically rather than because anyone decided to spend more on each child. The policy question it raises has arguments on both sides: a school that loses 40 students does not lose 40 students' worth of fixed costs, since it still needs a principal, a payroll secretary, a boiler and a full slate of course offerings. Whether that justifies indefinite protection, and for how long, is a judgment this page does not make. For scale, the FY2026 total of about $388 million is a little over 1 percent of DOE's $34.5 billion in FY2025 operating spending.

5. The class size mandate and its extension

A 2022 state law requires smaller classes in New York City schools. DOE projects that full compliance will cost $949 million to $1.7 billion a year in added teacher salaries, and the School Construction Authority projects about $18 billion in construction. In 2026 the Mamdani administration sought more time, and in June the state extended the deadline by two years, to the 2029-30 school year.

What the law requires

Chapter 556 of the Laws of 2022, signed Sept. 8, 2022, caps class sizes in New York City schools and requires the district to publish annual class size reduction plans. The caps are well below the limits in the contract with the United Federation of Teachers (UFT) that governed before:

The original law phased compliance in by share of classes: 20 percent in 2023-24, 40 percent in 2024-25, 60 percent in 2025-26, 80 percent in 2026-27 and 100 percent in 2027-28. Chapter 155 of the Laws of 2026, signed June 26, 2026, extended the schedule: after the first three years, compliance now rises 10 points a year, to 70 percent in 2026-27, 80 percent in 2027-28, 90 percent in 2028-29 and 100 percent in 2029-30. Exemptions are limited to statutory categories, such as lack of space or a shortage of teachers in a license area, and must be approved by the chancellor and the presidents of the UFT and the Council of School Supervisors and Administrators (CSA).

Where compliance stands

DOE reported that 46 percent of classes were at or below the caps in 2024-25, above the 40 percent requirement, and 64 percent in 2025-26, above the 60 percent requirement (as of Oct. 31, 2025). DOE says these counts reflect non-exempted classes; the City Council counted 10,535 classes with approved exemptions in 2025-26. For 2025-26, more than 800 schools submitted plans, and in April 2025 DOE notified schools of funding for 3,700 teachers and more than 100 assistant principals, expecting to spend over $400 million at roughly 750 schools.

What it is projected to cost

The Independent Budget Office estimated in July 2023 that full compliance would require about 17,700 additional teachers at $1.6 billion to $1.9 billion a year. Its December 2025 update, using 2023-24 data, put the need at 16,300 teachers at $1.5 billion to $1.7 billion a year. DOE's November 2025 financial impact statement projects $949 million to $1.7 billion a year in added teacher salaries beyond what it had already distributed. Capital costs are larger and less certain: the School Construction Authority estimated $22.3 billion to $26.8 billion in 2024, according to the city comptroller, and about $18 billion in DOE's November 2025 statement.

In the city's FY2027 budget, the Executive Plan in May 2026 replaced most of the new class size money the preliminary budget had proposed with "cost containment" savings ($508 million in FY2027), leaving a $122 million year-over-year increase that the City Council estimated would pay for about 1,000 more teachers. The adopted budget added $122 million a year in state funding for class size starting in FY2027; the comptroller counts $914 million a year in class size funding added by the Mamdani administration by FY2030.

Reported figures, not primary-verified

From Chalkbeat's April 2, 2026, report, "Mamdani campaigned on fulfilling NYC's class size mandate. So why is he pushing for a delay?": the mayor's preliminary budget included $543 million in additional city funding for class size in the coming fiscal year and $943 million in each of the three years after. State Sen. John Liu, the law's sponsor, was open to stretching the remaining phase-in to four years rather than two, with about 70 percent compliance in September and 10 more points each year, and said, "Adjustments in the timeline are not meant to provide fiscal relief." The state Assembly proposed $600 million in state support. The enacted outcome is described above.

How this interacts with everything above. The class size mandate pushes in the opposite direction from enrollment decline. Falling enrollment raises per-pupil spending without raising total spending; the mandate raises total spending by requiring more teachers for the same students. Both are happening at once, which is part of why New York City's per-pupil figure is hard to read as a simple measure of generosity or of waste. Smaller classes also mean fewer students per teacher, one of the documented differences between New York City and the national average listed in Section 10; the mandate widens that difference by design.

If you're looking for a big central-bureaucracy story, the operating budget isn't it. 10. Why is New York City so much higher?

In FY2024, New York City spent $35,796 per pupil against a U.S. national average of $17,619 (Census current spending, dollars as spent), a gap of $18,177. A common explanation is that the city serves more students with disabilities. The data confirms that its share is high. What the data does not support is a confident claim about how much of the gap that explains.

Part B: Why this page does not put a number on each cause

An earlier version of this page carried a bar chart that split New York City's roughly $18,000 per-pupil gap over the national average into dollar amounts for special education mix, staffing levels and teacher pay. That chart has been removed. Producing it required assigning a fully loaded cost per teacher and an excess cost per special education student, and no official source publishes either figure for New York City. The dollar splits were therefore assumptions presented as findings.

What follows instead is what official sources do document, without an attempt to apportion the gap among causes. No government source publishes a decomposition of why New York City's per-pupil spending exceeds the national average, and this page does not manufacture one.

Census notes that New York City reports guidance counselors under instruction, which slightly overstates its instruction spending. Census figures are not adjusted for regional differences in wages or prices.

What can be said plainly: New York City staffs its schools more intensively than the national norm, serves a substantially higher share of students with disabilities than the state or the country and operates an old building stock in a high-cost region. Each of those raises cost. How much each contributes to the gap is not established by any published government analysis, and the honest answer is that the split is unknown.

11. How New York City compares to other large districts

Among the 100 largest U.S. school systems, New York City had the highest current spending per pupil in FY2024, the most recent year the Census Bureau has published. It ranked first in every year from FY2016 through FY2024 except FY2021, when Boston, then among the 100 largest, ranked first ($31,397 to New York City's $29,931).
