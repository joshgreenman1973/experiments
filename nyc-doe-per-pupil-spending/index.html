1. The long climb - district schools only, apples-to-apples

One consistent methodology: U.S. Census F-33 current spending per pupil for the NYC school district (Geographic Districts 1-32 plus District 75). This is the only NYC per-pupil series with a uniform definition across years. It excludes charter schools (charter students and charter dollars are both out of scope) and excludes capital outlay and debt service. Toggle between per-pupil and total district current spending.

Per-pupil, district only. Charter schools are separate local education agencies under New York law and Census excludes them. Federal pandemic relief (ESSER) inflated FY2022; as that money wound down, the district-only per-pupil figure fell from $35,914 to $33,387 in FY2023 before returning to $35,796 in FY2024. Both the rise and the fall are in the published Census series. Nominal dollars. None of these figures are inflation-adjusted. Consumer prices rose substantially over this period, so the real increase is considerably smaller than the nominal one shown. Charters excluded. 2. Why the line goes up: it's a denominator story too

Per-pupil is a ratio, and both parts move. Between FY2016 and FY2024, Census-reported NYC district enrollment fell from 981,667 to 845,509 (-13.9%), while total district current spending rose from $26.26B to $34.97B (+33.2%). Published per-pupil spending rose 48.5% over the same period - faster than spending itself, because the student count was shrinking underneath it.

Sources, both read directly from the U.S. Census Bureau Annual Survey of School System Finances, NYC row, each year's own release: total current spending from Table 16 ("Expenditure of the 100 Largest Public Elementary-Secondary School Systems," "Current spending" column, thousands converted to billions); enrollment from Table 18, which Census footnotes as fall membership collected by NCES on the Common Core of Data agency universe file, with charter schools excluded. Neither series is derived from the other. Nominal dollars, not inflation-adjusted.
The gap between the two growth rates is the denominator effect. Spending grew 33.2%; per-pupil grew 48.5%. Arithmetically, that means the pupil base Census used for its per-pupil calculation fell about 10% over the period. So a meaningful share of "NYC's per-pupil spending is rising" reflects fewer students rather than more dollars - a pattern common to districts losing enrollment nationwide.
Two caveats on this comparison. First, the fall-membership count Census publishes (down 13.9%) is not the same as the pupil base Census uses to compute per-pupil amounts (down about 10%); the agency does not publish the latter directly, and this page does not attempt to reconcile them. Second, all figures are nominal. Because consumer prices rose substantially between FY2016 and FY2024, the real, inflation-adjusted increase in both spending and per-pupil spending is materially smaller than the nominal figures shown here.

3. The charter sector, which the spending data leaves out

The Census series above excludes charter schools entirely. They are separate local education agencies under New York law, but they enroll New York City children and are funded with public money, so leaving them out understates how many students the city's public system serves. Between 2012-13 and 2025-26, NYC charter enrollment grew from 58,493 to 149,879 (+156%) across 159 to 285 schools, while district enrollment fell from 985,388 to 810,653 (-17.7%).

Source: New York State Education Department, BEDS Day enrollment files (Student Information Repository System), school-level "All Students" tables, each school year's own release. Figures are the sum of PreK-12 enrollment for all schools located in the five New York City counties (Bronx, Kings, New York, Queens, Richmond), split by NYSED's own "School Type" field, which classifies each school as Public or Charter. Stacked areas sum to total NYC public school enrollment including charters.
Counting both sectors changes the size of the decline, but does not explain it. From 2012-13 to 2025-26 the district lost 174,735 students while charters gained 91,386. Adding the two together, total NYC public enrollment fell 8.0% rather than the 17.7% the district figure alone shows. That is an arithmetic point about which students the Census series counts - not a finding that charter growth caused the district decline.
Why this chart cannot tell you where students went. The two lines are counts, not flows. A child enrolling in a charter kindergarten was never in a district school to begin with, so charter growth is not the same as students transferring out of the district. NYC district enrollment also fell for reasons this chart does not measure at all - fewer births in the city, families moving out, and shifts to private schooling or homeschooling. No source on this page apportions the district decline among those causes, and this page does not attempt to.

Comparability caveats. These NYSED counts are BEDS Day (fall snapshot) enrollment and do not match the Census F-33 enrollment used in the charts above, which comes from the NCES Common Core of Data; the two collections use different dates and definitions, so the district totals differ by roughly 10,000-30,000 students in any given year. NYSED school years are labeled by span (2025-26); Census fiscal years are labeled by end year. This chart shows enrollment only. It does not show charter per-pupil spending, which comes from a different funding stream and is not reported in the Census district series or in the IBO breakdowns below.

4. Hold harmless: when enrollment falls but the budget doesn't

The two sections above show enrollment falling. This section is about what the city decided to do with school budgets while that happened. Under NYC's Fair Student Funding formula, a school's budget follows its students - fewer students should mean a smaller allocation. Since the pandemic, the city has largely suspended that consequence. The policy is called "hold harmless," and it is one reason per-pupil spending has climbed even at schools that are shrinking.

How it works, step by step

  1. In spring, every school is given a budget for the coming year based on projected enrollment, run through the Fair Student Funding formula (a base amount per student, plus weights for grade level, poverty, English language learners, and students with disabilities).
  2. In the fall, actual enrollment is counted on register date.
  3. Normally, mid-year the budget is trued up. Schools that enrolled more students than projected get more money; schools that enrolled fewer have money taken back. That taking-back is the "clawback."
  4. Under hold harmless, the clawback is switched off. A school that projected 400 students and enrolled 340 keeps the budget it was given for 400. The schools that gained students still get their increase - the adjustment only runs one way.
  5. The gap between what the formula says the school should get and what it actually keeps is the hold harmless cost, and it is paid for centrally.
The mechanism matters more than the label. Hold harmless does not add a line to a school's budget marked "hold harmless." It removes a subtraction. That is why a school can be described as receiving millions in hold harmless funding while its principal experiences it simply as the budget not being cut.

What official sources say it costs

The clearest government figure comes from the Department of Education's own class size plan, which states that New York City has invested a total of $1.2 billion since FY2021 in "hold harmless" funding for schools losing enrollment to ensure they can maintain services (NYC Public Schools, SY2025-2026 Class Size Reduction Plan). The policy has been renewed repeatedly: Chancellor Aviles-Ramos announced a mid-year hold harmless in November 2024, and it has been extended again since.

Reported figures, not primary-verified

The rest of this section comes from Chalkbeat's June 2026 analysis of Department of Education enrollment and budget data, "Hold harmless costs grow as enrollment losses continue." The underlying data is the DOE's, but the aggregation and the school-level figures are Chalkbeat's, and this page has not independently reproduced them from source files. Every other number on this page comes straight from a government document; these do not, and are marked accordingly because the school-level detail is not published in a form this page could verify.

  • About $290 million in the 2025-26 school year, more than double the prior annual level.
  • 723 schools received hold harmless funds; 55 received more than $1 million each.
  • Nearly $1.9 billion cumulatively since 2020.

Two concrete schools:

  • I.S. 339, Bronx. Enrollment fell from 315 students in 2020 to about 150 today - less than half. The school receives roughly $2.5 million in hold harmless funding, close to a third of its $7.8 million total budget. Put differently: about one dollar in three at this school is money the funding formula says it should no longer be getting.
  • Urban Assembly Institute of Math and Science for Young Women, Downtown Brooklyn. Enrollment fell from nearly 500 in 2020 to about 350. It is slated to receive roughly $374,000 - a much smaller cushion, because it lost a much smaller share of its students.

These totals do not agree with each other, and the page is not resolving that. DOE says $1.2 billion since FY2021. Chalkbeat says nearly $1.9 billion since 2020. Testimony from the Citizens Budget Commission put the 2025-26 figure near $400 million rather than $290 million. The windows differ, the definitions of what counts as hold harmless differ, and no single published document reconciles them.

Why this belongs on a per-pupil page. Per-pupil spending is spending divided by students. Hold harmless holds the numerator steady at schools where the denominator is falling, which pushes per-pupil spending up at exactly those schools - mechanically, not because anyone decided to spend more on each child. The policy question it raises is a genuine one with arguments on both sides: a school that loses 40 students does not lose 40 students' worth of fixed costs, since it still needs a principal, a payroll secretary, a boiler, and a full slate of course offerings. Whether that justifies indefinite protection, and for how long, is a judgment this page does not make.

5. The class size mandate and its postponement

The largest identified future driver of NYC school spending is a state law requiring smaller classes. It is being phased in now, its cost estimates run into the billions annually, and in 2026 the Mamdani administration sought to slow it down.

What the law requires

Chapter 556 of the Laws of 2022, signed in September 2022, caps class sizes in New York City schools and requires the district to publish a five-year reduction plan. The caps are well below the limits in the teachers' union contract that governed before:

Compliance phases in by share of classes: 20% in SY2023-24, 40% in SY2024-25, 60% in SY2025-26, 80% in SY2026-27, and 100% in SY2027-28. The law includes statutory exemption categories; a class can exceed the cap only when the circumstances fit one of those categories and the Chancellor, the UFT president, and the CSA president all agree.

Where compliance stands

DOE reports it exceeded the 40% benchmark for SY2024-25, reaching 46% of classes at or below the caps. For SY2025-26, the 60% year, DOE received over 800 school applications under a school-driven planning model and in April notified schools of funding for 3,700 teachers and more than 100 assistant principals, saying it expected to spend over $400 million on class size reduction that year. The steep part of the curve is still ahead: the jump to 80%, then to universal compliance.

What it is projected to cost

The Independent Budget Office has estimated that full compliance would require as many as 17,700 additional teachers, at an annual operating cost of roughly $1.6 billion to $1.9 billion. Capital costs are far more uncertain, because most schools lack the physical rooms for more, smaller classes; published construction estimates range from the high teens of billions into the tens of billions, and this page does not pick among them.

Reported figures, not primary-verified

From Chalkbeat's April 2026 reporting, "Mamdani seeks class size law delay in budget negotiation": the Mamdani preliminary budget carried $543 million in additional city funding for the coming fiscal year and $943 million annually for each of the three years after, with officials projecting that full implementation could reach roughly $1.7 billion annually for teachers alone. The administration sought to stretch the compliance timeline, and State Senator John Liu, the law's sponsor, signaled openness to four years rather than two - roughly 70% compliance by September and an additional 10 points each year after - while stating that timeline adjustments were not intended to provide fiscal relief. The state Assembly proposed $600 million in state support. These are budget-negotiation figures reported at the time; the enacted outcome is not reflected here.

How this interacts with everything above. The class size mandate pushes in the opposite direction from enrollment decline. Enrollment falling raises per-pupil spending without raising total spending; the mandate raises total spending by requiring more teachers for the same students. Both are live at once, which is part of why NYC's per-pupil figure is hard to read as a simple measure of generosity or of waste. Note also that smaller classes mean a lower pupil-to-teacher ratio, which Section 10 lists as one of the documented reasons NYC's per-pupil spending already exceeds the national average - the mandate deliberately widens that gap.

6. The $40 billion, broken down (IBO)

IBO groups the FY2024 $40B total into four buckets. The labels below describe what each bucket actually contains, rather than using IBO's internal accounting shorthand.

What each slice is. Direct school programs ($19B): money that funds teaching and learning - general ed school budgets, charter pass-through payments, Pre-K, special education instruction, and categorical grant programs. Staff health & welfare ($4.5B): employer share of health insurance and welfare fund for current DOE staff (budgeted centrally, not charged to schools - see note below). Central services & overhead ($9.5B): pupil transportation, school food, building operations, safety, and citywide administration - services schools consume but that aren't budgeted line-by-line to each school. Pensions & debt service ($7B): retirement contributions for current/former staff and principal + interest on capital debt; sits outside the DOE operating budget. Why there is no "general education per-pupil" figure here. A natural question is what the $8.4B general education schools line works out to per general-education student. This page does not publish that number, because doing so requires a matching enrollment denominator - general-education students in non-charter, non-District 75 school sites - that neither IBO nor DOE publishes alongside the spending figure. Dividing the $8.4B by any of the enrollment counts that are published would mix populations that do not correspond to the spending line, producing a number that looks precise but is not verifiable against any source document.

Why staff benefits ($3.5B) are a separate bar from schools ($14.7B). In the NYC city budget, health insurance and welfare-fund contributions for DOE employees are budgeted on a single centrally-held line item (the "Fringe Benefits" U/A) rather than charged to each school's budget. Economically, the overwhelming majority of that $3.5B is compensation for school-based staff - teachers, paraprofessionals, guidance counselors, school aides, principals - so it is effectively part of the cost of running schools. It is shown as its own bar because that's how it appears in the public budget document, but think of it as an extension of the "Schools" bar. Combined, school-based activity plus its associated staff benefits is roughly $18.2B, or about 71% of the operating budget.

If you're looking for a big central-bureaucracy story, the operating budget isn't it. Central administration is roughly 1.35% of the DOE operating budget. The much bigger cost categories are schools and their staff benefits (~71% combined), district-wide special education and non-public placements (13%), and operations like transportation, food, and facilities (14%). Pensions and debt service - a further ~$7B - sit outside the operating budget entirely.

What is NOT broken out publicly

  • District 75 per-pupil spending. District 75 serves ~24,000 students with the most significant disabilities across all five boroughs, and is funded outside the Fair Student Funding formula. DOE does not publish an annual District 75 per-pupil figure in its standard fiscal releases.
  • Carter vs. Connors, separately. The Department of Education has stated publicly that it cannot disaggregate Carter (private school tuition for students with disabilities) from Connors (nonpublic related services) spending.

10. Why is NYC so much higher?

In FY2024, NYC spent $35,796 per pupil against a U.S. national average of $17,619 (Census F-33, current spending) - a gap of $18,177. A common explanation is that NYC serves more students with disabilities. The data confirms that NYC's share is high. What the data does not support is a confident claim about how much of the gap that explains.

Part A: NYC does have a high share of students with disabilities

Part B: Why this page does not put a number on each cause

An earlier version of this page carried a bar chart that split NYC's roughly $18,000 per-pupil gap over the national average into dollar amounts for special education mix, staffing levels, and teacher pay. That chart has been removed. Producing it required assigning a fully-loaded cost per teacher and an excess cost per special education student, and no official source publishes either figure for New York City. The dollar splits were therefore assumptions dressed as findings, and they are not defensible.

What follows instead is the list of cost drivers that official sources do document, without an attempt to apportion the gap among them. No government source publishes a decomposition of why NYC's per-pupil spending exceeds the national average, and this page does not manufacture one.
