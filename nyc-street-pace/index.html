How this was made

The readers. The Department of Transportation reads E-ZPass tags at fixed points and times the same tags between pairs of readers. Each pair is a "link," usually one direction of a few blocks. The city publishes a rolling 15-minute median travel time and speed for each of 351 links, recomputed about once a minute, in one Open Data file per year. Most links are in Midtown Manhattan, where the readers were installed for the Midtown in Motion signal program; the rest are on parkways, bridges and arterials in Queens, Brooklyn, Staten Island and upper Manhattan. Only vehicles with E-ZPass tags are timed.

Clock change. Through May 16, 2025, the city stamped readings in Coordinated Universal Time; when the feed came back on June 30, 2025, after a six-week outage, it stamped them in New York time. The fastest hour of the night shows it: before the outage it falls at 8 or 9 UTC depending on daylight saving, and after it at 4 every month. The earlier readings were converted to New York time; the later ones were used as published.

What was kept. Rows with a full 15-minute window and at least three timed vehicles, at speeds above zero and under 100 feet per second (68 mph). About 45% of raw rows carry no reading at all and were dropped. Each link's speed for an hour of a day is the average of the medians in that hour; each link-hour-day counts once.

Periods. Congestion pricing began Jan. 5, 2025, in Manhattan south of 60th Street. To compare like seasons, each year on the map runs Feb. 1 to Oct. 31. The city's file has no readings at all from May 16 to June 30, 2025, so those same weeks are left out of every year. Weekdays exclude federal holidays, the day after Thanksgiving and Christmas Eve. A link's hourly value needs at least 20 days behind it. The "zone" is every link whose midpoint falls inside the MTA's published Central Business District geofence. Year-to-year comparisons use only links with weekday daytime (7 a.m. to 7 p.m.) values in 2024, 2025 and 2026. The monthly chart uses a fixed set of links reporting in at least 90% of months.

What this cannot tell you. Speeds changed for many reasons at once: tolls, signal timing, bus and bike lanes, construction, deliveries and the economy. The comparison to segments outside the zone helps but does not isolate the toll's effect. Full notes and code: methodology.
